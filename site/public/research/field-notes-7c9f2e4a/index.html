Mapping How a Medical AI Model Groups Skin-Lesion Images: A Careful Atlas With Firm Limits

Evidence status label: EXPLORATORY_ONLY

Researchers recorded where the MedSigLIP image encoder places each of 2,594 public dermoscopy photos relative to the others. The bookkeeping holds up under audit. Nobody tested what the resulting map means for patients or for diagnosis.

The question behind the project

Much of today's medical imaging AI starts with an encoder. This is a model that reads a picture and converts it into a long list of numbers, often called a representation or embedding. The model gives images it treats as alike similar lists of numbers. Other systems reuse these lists for classification, image search, and tools that combine pictures with text. That makes it useful to open the representation up and ask which images the model considers close together.

Project P01 built that kind of inspection tool, which the authors call an atlas, for MedSigLIP. MedSigLIP is a medically tuned image encoder that supplies the vision component of the MedGemma model family. The images come from the International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) 2018 challenge, specifically the training material for Task 2. That task contains dermoscopy photographs (magnified views of skin lesions) labeled for five dermoscopic features.

“Full cohort” has a narrow meaning here. It covers every one of the 2,594 rows in a single reconciled image list. It does not cover all 3,694 Task 2 images, every ISIC image, or a sample of patients. The authors present the work as a descriptive supplement to a larger research program. It is not a study of whether the model helps anyone make a diagnosis.

How the atlas was built

Each image was put into a standard 448-pixel format and passed through the vision component of one specific MedGemma 1.5 checkpoint. The model's final-layer outputs for an image were averaged into a single list of 1,152 numbers. That list was scaled to unit length and stored at reduced (16-bit) precision. The result is one point per image in a 1,152-dimensional space.

The atlas has two products. The first is a neighbor list. For every image, the software found the 20 other images whose vectors pointed in the most similar direction, measured by cosine similarity. Cosine similarity is a score that reaches 1 when two vectors point exactly the same way. The search was exact: it compared every image against every other rather than taking shortcuts, so the same inputs and software produce the same answer. The list holds 51,880 links. These links have three properties to keep in mind. They are directed, because A can appear on B's list without B appearing on A's. They are cut off, because only the top 20 are kept. They overlap, so they are not independent observations.

The second product is a principal component analysis (PCA). This technique compresses the 1,152 numbers into a handful of summary directions that capture as much of the variation between images as possible. The team kept 16 such directions. The software picks the method used to compute them, and here it chose a randomized one. A fixed seed (17) was set, but the coordinates could still shift under a different software version or computing environment.

The image list gave each image one of two stored roles: 699 marked training and 1,895 marked development. The atlas pooled both. That is acceptable for a description, but it means the atlas has “seen” images that other parts of the program hold back for evaluation. For that reason, the authors prohibit using P01 outputs to split data, tune models, choose thresholds or endpoints, pick example images for confirmatory figures, or relabel cases. A targeted search of the program's code and documents found no later analysis pointing at the P01 outputs. No formal dependency record exists, though, to prove that none ever used them.

The closest-neighbor similarity sounds striking: nearly every image has a neighbor it matches at about 0.999. The number is hard to interpret, though. No one measured what random pairs of images score, or what verified-related and verified-unrelated pairs score. Without that comparison, the value could come from several things. The model's representation space might simply be crowded in one direction. The averaging and preprocessing steps could contribute. Dermoscopy photos share a lot of visual structure. The reduced storage precision could play a part. Undetected copies or repeat photos of the same lesion or patient could too. The atlas cannot tell these explanations apart.

The group check produced a clear negative finding. The image list already marked 35 groups of related images. If the neighbor list worked as a duplicate finder, members of these groups should turn up on each other's lists. Only 36 of the 90 grouped images had a group-mate among their top 20, and 54 had none. Sixteen of the 35 groups produced no such link at all. The atlas should not be used to find duplicates. A separate human review effort in the program (project P02) remains the route for that question, and it has not been completed: its list of accepted duplicate pairs is still empty.

Two fields that look like passes but are not

The first version of the atlas's quality report included a “recall” check showing a score of 1.0 across 1,000 queries at depth 50, marked as passed. Recall checks matter when a fast, approximate search is compared against an exact one. Here the search was exact, and the code wrote those numbers as fixed constants. No comparison ever took place. During the 28 August revision the authors reclassified the fields as a not-applicable placeholder. A 12 September note says the current code emits an explicit placeholder marker, and the original report was kept unchanged.

Similarly, the run's receipt contains the literal field “certifiable: true.” That field describes the receipt's structure. It is not a judgment that the results are scientifically certified, and no result certificate exists.

Limitations

  • Patient and lesion identity is unknown for most images, so the images cannot be treated as independent people or lesions.
  • Duplicate review is unfinished, and unrecognized repeat images may inflate the similarity scores.
  • The analysis uses one model checkpoint, one image format, one averaged representation and one storage precision.
  • Whether these exact images were in the encoder's own training data is unresolved.
  • There was no diagnostic endpoint, no clinician judgment of neighbor relevance, and no external or prospective cohort.
  • Key artifacts cannot currently be checked by an outside reviewer.

Why it matters anyway

P01 is a completed audit of a map, not a discovery about skin disease. It shows that every image in one well-defined list received a representation, and that the neighbor and coordinate tables are complete and internally consistent. It also tested the obvious shortcut, “close neighbors are duplicates,” and found that shortcut unreliable. Other researchers building on medical image encoders often skip that step. The main lesson is to report similarity maps together with what they cannot show.

Catching Look-Alike Skin Images Before They Skew an AI Test

A dermatology imaging program built software to flag repeated and related images in public ISIC dermoscopy data. The tools ran and later produced a deliberately cautious split between training and evaluation images. Which flagged pairs are genuine duplicates is still unconfirmed.

Evidence status: METHODS_AUDIT_ONLY

The problem: one mole, many rows

Researchers who train an AI to read dermoscopy images (magnified photographs of skin spots) normally hold some images back to test it. That test is fair only if the held-back images are genuinely new to the model. Public collections such as the International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) archive are assembled from many sources, and one mole can appear more than once. It may have been photographed from another angle, recompressed, cropped, or imaged on a different day. One patient may also contribute several different lesions. If one copy ends up in training and its twin ends up in the test set, the model can look better than it really is. This is called leakage.

Project P02 built tools to find these relationships and keep related images on the same side of the train/test divide. The write-up reviewed here is a methods audit. It asks what the tools did, what their saved outputs show, and what they cannot yet prove. It does not evaluate diagnosis, model accuracy, or patient care.

How the system is designed

Picture the collection as a map in which every image is a dot and each possible relationship is a line between two dots. The software proposes lines using several kinds of evidence, which range from strong to weak:

  • Exact matches. Identical files, or identical pixels after the image is decoded.
  • Perceptual fingerprints. A 64-bit summary of each image's coarse pattern, called a pHash. Two images whose fingerprints differ in only a few bits are flagged.
  • Embedding similarity. An AI model converts each image into a list of numbers, and pairs with very high similarity are flagged. In the test run the cutoff was a cosine similarity of at least 0.985, with up to 20 neighbors per image. Different lesions that happen to look alike can also score high.
  • A geometric check. For a fingerprint candidate, the software matches distinctive points between the two images and tries to warp one onto the other. The pair passes only if there are at least 8 good matches, at least half of them fit a consistent geometry, the warped image covers at least a quarter of the other, and the two look structurally similar (a similarity score of at least 0.75). The test has to pass in both directions, so a small crop cannot match a whole image in only one direction.
  • Identity records. Patient and lesion ID numbers, where the source provided them.
  • Human review. A person looks at a pair and names the relationship. The plan required a second, independent reviewer and a separate tie-breaker.

Exact matches and pairs that passed the two-way geometric check could be grouped automatically. Every other pair went to a review queue. The authors are careful to separate two things: the software grouping a pair, and a human confirming that the two images show the same lesion. The engineers set the thresholds by hand and never calibrated them against known answers. They are engineering settings, not measured error rates.

Results, step by step

An early candidate run

The first run processed 1,670 images and wrote 77 candidate pairs, all from perceptual fingerprints. The geometric check was switched off, so nothing was grouped automatically and all 77 pairs went to review. The run's record lacks the code version and the fingerprints of its inputs, and no tamper-evident receipt came with it. Every image carried the split label "unlabeled," so the run's report of zero train/test violations could never have detected one. These are 77 candidates, not 77 duplicates.

A controlled technical smoke test

A later version of the code, identified by an exact commit, ran on 668 images. It wrote 6,541 candidate pairs. Four pairs had both a fingerprint signal and an embedding signal and passed the two-way geometric check, so the software grouped them as near-duplicate transformations. The remaining 6,537 pairs were flagged by embedding similarity alone and stayed in the queue. The run's receipt labels it a technical smoke test that is not certifiable and may not be used for claims.

The audit notes two further gaps. First, no one independently checked the four software-grouped pairs. Second, the code checked only that the embedding table had the right number of rows, not that each row belonged to the right image. Related input lists contained the same 668 image IDs in the same order, which is reassuring but indirect. The exact embedding file was not re-examined. All 668 images were in the training role, so this run's zero violations are also uninformative. The code currently in the repository uses a different, one-direction verifier and does not reproduce the method that was run.

The identity map: a retraction, then a fix

The first identity map was withdrawn because blank ID fields had been read as real IDs, which can falsely link unrelated images. Version 2 covers 3,694 images from the ISIC 2018 Task 2 attribute-detection release (2,594 training, 100 validation, 1,000 test). It qualified 487 images with enough identity information and marked 3,207 as unverifiable, forming 3,372 components.

Human review stopped after the first reviewer

The first reviewer recorded 15 decisions: 14 pairs were judged similar but different lesions, and 1 was judged a different view of the same lesion. No second reviewer or tie-breaker followed, so the list of human-accepted links is empty. The 14-to-1 split is a useful reminder that visual similarity is not identity. With only 15 pairs, none chosen at random, it is not an accuracy estimate.

A frozen split that keeps known groups together

The frozen 2,594-image partition places 699 images in training and 1,895 in a fixed evaluation set. In the files these roles are stored as "TRAIN" and, confusingly, "DEV." The partition has 2,539 groups. Thirty-five of them hold more than one image, 90 images in total, and every one comes from ID records: 12 lesion-only groups with 27 images and 23 patient-and-lesion groups with 63 images. No group appears in both roles. That check is real but narrow. It covers only relationships already written into the group IDs and does not use the candidate graph at all.

Later: a deliberately cautious regrouping

After the manuscript's 28 August 2026 evidence cutoff, the program adopted a precautionary policy. Instead of waiting for every look-alike pair to be adjudicated, it keeps together any pair that matches exactly, shares a patient or lesion ID, falls within the fingerprint radius, or passes the geometric check. Pairs flagged only by embedding similarity stay audit-only. This run quarantined 143 unique fingerprint relationships (drawn from 224 raw rows) and kept 6,533 unique embedding-only relationships (from 6,537 rows) for audit. The 2,594 images formed 2,440 cautious groups, and 16 of those groups straddled training and evaluation. Moving each straddling group entirely into training moved 51 images, which took training from 699 to 750 and evaluation from 1,895 to 1,844.

The run also turned up a surprise. Under the current image decoder, the pixel-level hashes for all 2,594 publication images differed from their historical values, so they were recomputed under a single decoder. Exact-pixel comparisons therefore depend on the software version. The 12 September write-up re-checked the saved output files and their row relationships but did not decode the images again.

Limitations to keep in mind

  • No one knows how many true duplicates the candidate search misses, so none of the counts estimates how common duplicates are.
  • Thresholds were never calibrated, and the fingerprint radius changed between runs (6 in the early run, 4 in the smoke test).
  • Most images lack usable patient or lesion identity (3,207 of 3,694 in the identity map).
  • Human review is incomplete. The cautious policy may group too much, and it cannot catch relationships that no signal flagged.
  • Key raw outputs sit on a private computing cluster and cannot be examined independently.
  • The revised manuscript predates the cautious regrouping, so the project's documents need to be reconciled.
  • The ethics, source-licensing, and funding statements are still placeholders.

Why this matters

Many dermatology AI results depend on the assumption that the test images are new to the model. This audit shows concretely how that assumption can fail without anyone noticing. A check can report zero leakage when it had no way to detect any. A missing-value bug can invent identity links. A software grouping is not the same as a human confirmation. What the project honestly offers is working infrastructure and a precautionary split, not a finding about how many duplicates exist. That is still worth having, because it spells out what the next team must finish before anyone can call an ISIC test set clean: calibrated thresholds, a blinded second review, recovered identities, and evidence that outsiders can check.

The question in plain terms

Dermatologists examine moles through a dermoscope, a lighted magnifier, and look for named patterns. This study used five of them:

  • pigment network, a net-like pattern of pigment;
  • negative network, its paler inverse;
  • streaks;
  • small round globules;
  • tiny whitish structures called milia-like cysts.

The image collection includes labels saying which images show each pattern.

A modern medical image model turns each picture into long lists of numbers. It computes them in stages called layers, and none of the numbers comes with a meaning attached. A standard way to ask what they contain is a linear probe: a deliberately simple classifier that tries to tell labeled images from unlabeled ones using only a weighted sum of one layer's numbers. If the probe succeeds, the label is "linearly decodable" at that layer, meaning the information is laid out in an accessible way. That is all it means. Finding a pattern with a probe no more shows the model relies on it than finding an address stored in a phone shows the phone drove there.

This project probed a frozen medical vision encoder (one that was not retrained) at five sampled layers, numbered 0, 6, 13, 20 and 26, for those five labels.

How the study was built

The images were split into a fitting set of 750 and an evaluation set of 1,844. The same patient or lesion can appear in more than one photo, so images judged possibly related were bundled into groups: 641 groups in the fitting set and 1,799 in the evaluation set. A group was never split across training and testing folds. These groups are a cautious quarantine, not confirmed patient identities.

A panel is one combination of layer, label, arm (described in the next section) and random seed (17, 29 or 43). For each panel, the team:

  • trained logistic-regression probes using five outer and four inner cross-validation folds;
  • chose among three regularization strengths (0.01, 0.1 and 1) using fitting data only;
  • averaged the outer models' scores on the evaluation images.

Five layers, five labels, three arms and three seeds give 225 panels. The final layer was named in advance as the headline; it was not picked for looking best.

Two decoys to keep the probe honest

A flexible enough probe can find patterns even in noise, so every real analysis was paired with two decoys.

  • Random features. The model's numbers were swapped for random numbers of the same length. A probe here should score near chance.
  • Permuted labels. The real numbers were kept, but labels were shuffled among fitting groups of the same size, which breaks the true link between image and label. This probe should also land near chance.

The authors say plainly that three shuffles are a descriptive check. They are not a formal statistical test and cannot produce a p-value.

Checking the arithmetic, not just the output

Everything was saved: every fitted coefficient, fold assignment, selection score and prediction. An audit then:

  • rebuilt the evaluation predictions from the saved coefficients and the original inputs;
  • confirmed that no group was split across folds;
  • recomputed the scores from the raw rows.

All 225 panels passed. On 12 September, a narrower check re-read the 45 final-layer evaluation panels. Each held 1,844 unique images in 1,799 groups, and the reported scores reproduced to within one part in a trillion. So the bookkeeping is sound. That is different from a new experiment, and different from independent scientific review.

What the numbers show

The main score is ROC-AUC. Pick one image that has the label and one that doesn't: AUC is the chance that the probe ranks the labeled image higher. A score of 0.50 is a coin flip; 1.00 is perfect.

  • With real features, every label scored well above the random-feature decoy, which stayed between 0.465 and 0.517 throughout. Pigment network and streaks scored highest and milia-like cyst lowest.
  • Real-feature scores barely changed from seed to seed. That shows the procedure is stable, not that the finding would repeat on other images. Seeds are not new patients.

Examples: 0.796 to 0.835 for pigment network (seed 17), 0.651 to 0.705 for milia-like cyst (seed 29), and 0.792 to 0.876 for streaks (seed 29), which had the widest ranges. The streak puzzle

The most instructive result is a flaw the team found in its own control. For streaks, the shuffled-label decoy scored 0.633, 0.705 and 0.731, well above chance.

The cause they found lies in how the shuffle works: it can only swap labels between groups of the same size. Ten streak-positive labels sat in groups whose sizes (9, 13, 16 and 21 images) had no match, so those labels could never move. Of the 25 positive streak labels in the fitting data, 10, 10 and 11 stayed positive across the three seeds.

For streaks, then, the "shuffled" decoy was only partly shuffled. The team calls this incomplete randomization and does not claim it explains the whole elevated score. There are two practical consequences:

  • the real-versus-decoy gap for streaks is narrower than the real score alone suggests;
  • streak results rest on few positive examples.

The decoys cannot be treated as a clean chance baseline for every feature.

How this version relates to the earlier one

The project has a history, and older and newer numbers should not be mixed.

  1. Earlier retrospective run. A single-seed analysis (cluster job 45495, code commit 45613a5) evaluated 1,895 images in 1,876 groups, with neither decoy. Its prediction ledger was complete: 47,375 of 47,375 expected rows. Patient identity, however, could not be verified for 1,645 of the 1,895 evaluation images. A resampling analysis added afterward, in late August, produced tail fractions that the team states are not p-values.
  2. 28 August 2026. Stricter input checks were added, and a conservative policy for quarantining possibly related images was applied to the data partition.
  3. 8 September 2026. The current successor ran on the repaired partition (750 fitting and 1,844 evaluation images) with three seeds and both decoys, and passed the full replay audit.
  4. 12 September 2026. An editorial draft for the parent paper re-read the final-layer panels and reproduced the streak label-retention counts.

The partitions differ, so earlier and current scores are not interchangeable. Some of the project's status notes still list "single seed" and "no controls" as open problems. Those entries describe the earlier run and were addressed by the successor; the other limitations still apply.

What this does not show

  • Independent testing. The evaluation images had been used in earlier analyses.
  • Patient independence. The groups are cautious bundles, and identity information is incomplete.
  • That the model uses these features. A readable label could reflect the feature itself, features that tend to appear alongside it, how images were acquired, or how annotators labeled them.
  • A layer where features "emerge". Only five layers were sampled, and they were not formally compared.
  • Diagnostic accuracy, clinical usefulness or generalization to other clinics. It is also unknown whether the encoder saw these images during pretraining.
  • Statistical confirmation. There are no calibrated p-values, and the intervals are pointwise and conditional.

Why it matters

Probing is one of the main ways researchers try to understand what medical image models encode, and it is easy to over-read. This project is worth studying less for its headline numbers than for its method:

  • it named the headline layer in advance;
  • it used two different decoys;
  • it saved every intermediate result, then rebuilt the scores from scratch.

That method caught a real problem: a shuffle that could not move some labels. Without that care, the problem would have looked like a chance baseline. A control that partly fails, reported plainly, is useful evidence in its own right.

Stronger claims would require:

  • a fresh, better-identified image set;
  • a protocol planned with enough statistical power;
  • a shuffle that can move every label;
  • independent statistical and dermatology review.

Until then, the finding stands as a well-audited description of one model, five labels and one reused collection of images.

Does a Medical AI's View of Skin Lesions Line Up With What Dermatologists Mark?

A corrected rerun, and why its answer stays provisional. The first analysis graded its answers with the same expert outlines it had used to tune itself, so its result was invalidated. A rerun fixed that flaw and gave consistent, carefully rechecked patterns. It was scored on images the project had already seen, though, so it counts only as exploration and cannot confirm anything.

The question in plain terms

MedSigLIP is the image-reading part of Google's MedGemma medical AI models. In this project, each dermoscopy image (a magnified photo of a skin lesion) was resized to 448 pixels. The model then turned it into a 64 by 64 grid of small patches, each described by 1,152 numbers. These patch descriptions are called tokens.

The project asked a narrow question. If the model is frozen and a very simple readout is trained on its tokens, can that readout find where dermatologists outlined specific structures? There were five structures, all from the public ISIC 2018 challenge:

  • pigment network, a brown honeycomb mesh;
  • negative network, a paler inverse mesh;
  • streaks, which are radial lines;
  • globules, which are round dots;
  • milia-like cysts, which are small pale spots.

A cheap strategy can look like success. Some structures tend to sit near a lesion's centre, spread across it or hug its border. So the token readout was compared with four simple "spatial controls" that know only position or lesion shape:

  • a blob centred on the image;
  • a flat score over the whole lesion;
  • a score along the lesion's edge;
  • a small learned model that sees only lesion geometry.

If the tokens carry structure-specific information, the readout should beat all four.

What went wrong the first time

The original analysis trained on 699 images and evaluated on 1,895. Training a readout means choosing when to stop, called checkpoint selection. In the original analysis, that choice was made using the same 1,895 expert outlines that were later used to grade the results. It is like letting a student choose which practice answers to hand in after seeing the answer key.

Two independent reviewers identified the problem. A code check on 28 August 2026 confirmed that the evaluation outlines had selected stopping epochs 66, 85 and 55 for the linear readouts and 25, 22 and 26 for the small nonlinear ones. The original analysis had reported four of five structures clearing a "three of five" bar. That count is now void.

Two other historical items are also set aside:

  • A results field reading "eligible confirmatory" turned out to be written by the code no matter what the results were.
  • An even earlier run was logged as successful but produced no usable output.

These records are kept only as an audit trail.

How the rerun was built

The rerun, completed on 6 September 2026, separated fitting from evaluation.

First, images were divided under a conservative grouping rule. Images known or suspected to be related, such as near-duplicates, were kept on the same side. This put 51 images from the old evaluation set into the fitting set. The result was 750 fitting images in 641 related groups and 1,844 evaluation images in 1,799 groups, with no group crossing sides.

Three kinds of readout were then trained with three random seeds each:

  • a linear readout, the main model;
  • a small nonlinear readout;
  • the geometry-only control.

Stopping points were chosen using only held-back slices of the fitting images. The models were then locked. A second computing job, allowed to start only after the first finished successfully, used the locked models to score the 1,844 evaluation images. Analysis followed from the saved scores.

One small caveat about timing. Before fitting, a routine check confirmed that the evaluation files existed and had the right shapes. So the lock did not come before every contact with evaluation data. No evaluation outlines or scores were used to choose models, however.

How the measurement works

For each image that contains a given structure, the readout scores every grid cell. Average precision (AP) then asks how well cells inside the expert outline are ranked above cells outside it, with 1 meaning a perfect ranking. The expert outlines were shrunk to the same 64 by 64 grid, and a cell counted as positive if any part of the outline touched it.

For each image, the control's AP was subtracted from the readout's AP. These differences were averaged within each group of related images, and each group then counted equally. To gauge uncertainty, the groups were resampled 2,000 times (a bootstrap). That produced a 95% range for each of the 20 structure-and-control pairings.

Three features of these ranges matter:

  • Each range is computed separately (pointwise), with no correction for making 20 comparisons.
  • They treat the trained models as fixed, so they ignore variation from retraining.
  • AP is measured only on images where the structure is present. It shows how well the readout finds the structure within the image, not whether it can tell if the structure is there at all.

What still limits the conclusions

This project is a worked example of how a plausible positive result can dissolve under scrutiny. It also shows what an honest repair looks like. The fix cannot manufacture a fresh test set, however. A trustworthy answer needs four more things:

  • images never used before;
  • tests showing the gain is specific to MedSigLIP's representation;

The question behind the experiment

When dermatologists read dermoscopy images (magnified, evenly lit views of skin lesions), they look for named structures. Roughly speaking, a pigment network is a grid of brown lines, globules are round dots, streaks are lines at the lesion edge, and milia-like cysts are small pale bright spots. The ISIC 2018 challenge released expert-drawn masks showing where such attributes appear in each image.

MedSigLIP is a medically tuned image encoder from the MedGemma model family: it converts an image into a long list of numbers. An earlier project, P03, fitted simple linear probes, lightweight scoring rules that read those numbers and output a score for whether an attribute is present. P05 asked a follow-up question. If you disrupt the exact region an expert marked as, say, pigment network, does the probe's score fall more than when you disrupt a similar-sized patch elsewhere in the same lesion?

That comparison is the core of the design. Any edit can shift a score simply because the picture now looks odd. Editing a matched "control" region in the same lesion is meant to cancel out that generic disturbance, so that any extra drop can be attributed to the targeted region. Even a flawless version of this test would show only that a score is sensitive to an edited region. It would not show that the encoder understands or relies on a medical concept.

The plan: a relay with two firewalls

P05 was designed as the third leg of a relay. P03 showed that attributes could be read out. P04 checked whether those readouts lined up spatially with the experts' masks, and only attributes that passed P04's gate were to go forward. P05 would then test those attributes on a separate, untouched image set called C2.

Two firewalls were essential. P04's evaluation masks must not be used to tune P04's own models, or its gate would be grading models that had been partly chosen using the answers. And P05 must use images that played no part in P04's selection.

What went wrong upstream

An independent review of the source code found that both firewalls failed. After each training epoch, P04 computed its loss on all 1,895 images in its nominal evaluation set, kept the best-scoring checkpoint, and then reported results on those same images. A comparison model was also stopped early using the same masks. That contaminated gate chose the four attributes P05 went on to test. The fifth attribute, negative network, was never run.

P05 then used exactly the same 1,895 records instead of a fresh cohort. An analogy: you pick which contestants advance by watching the final round, then judge the finalists on a replay of that same round. The judging can be arithmetically perfect and still tell you little about how they would do on a new stage.

The leak did not corrupt P05's own arithmetic, because P05 scored images with P03's fixed probes, not with P04's models. What failed was the justification for testing these four attributes, and the independence of the images they were tested on.

A short chronology

  1. Frozen abort. The project's official record was set to aborted, citing unmet preconditions: P04's gate had not properly passed, the separate C2 cohort had not been frozen, and no clinician realism protocol had been signed.
  2. Later developmental runs. Four P05 runs were then executed and logged as succeeded. Those execution logs do not override the abort.
  3. Independent review. Reviewers traced the P04 checkpoint-selection leak and added it as a further blocker. The report was rewritten as an invalidated audit.
  4. 6 September 2026. A successor P04 run restricted checkpoint selection to its fitting data. Its 1,844-image evaluation set had already been exposed, and its results are exploratory, so it does not reopen P05.
  5. 12 September 2026. P05 was closed by exclusion from the publication. Its files are kept so the history can be traced.

How the experiment was built

Finding a control region. For each image, the expert's attribute mask was shifted to a new spot inside the lesion. A candidate was accepted if it did not overlap the target, stayed within the lesion, had an area within ±15% of the target, and sat at a similar distance from the lesion's center (normalized difference of at most 0.10). Up to 500 attempts were allowed. The matcher did not balance local color, texture, distance from the lesion border, or whether the region was broken into fragments.

Four kinds of edit. The edits were Gaussian blur (σ = 4), shuffling 8×8-pixel patches, color noise drawn to resemble the region's own colors, and "feature-mean replacement." The last one swaps the encoder's internal tokens for the region with an average of nearby lesion tokens. It happens inside the model, so it never produces a real image that a dermatologist could inspect.

The measurement. For each image, probe fold and edit type, the analysis took the score change after editing the target minus the score change after editing the control. A negative value means the targeted edit lowered the score more. Values were averaged over matched images, and uncertainty intervals came from 2,000 resamples of groups of related images.

The pass rule as coded. An edit type "cleared" if its interval stayed entirely below zero in all five probe folds. An attribute passed if at least three of the four edit types cleared and blurring the whole lesion (σ = 6) lowered the average score in every fold. The whole-lesion blur was meant as a sanity check, but its expected direction and size were never established in advance.

Why these numbers cannot become findings

  • Chosen through a leak. The four attributes are the product of a selection that had seen its test answers. A clean upstream rerun might choose a different set.
  • Reused images. No untouched cohort was ever used.
  • Passes rest on subsets. Only 40.57% of pigment-network images and 57.20% of globule images got a matched control. The results describe only images where matching worked, and those may differ systematically from the rest.
  • Imperfect controls. Color, texture and border distance were not matched. The random draws for target and control edits were not paired. Failed patch shuffles meant that different edit types analyzed slightly different image sets.
  • Missing checks. There was no do-nothing processing control, the whole-lesion blur check was never validated, and the in-model token edit counted as much as the real pixel edits.
  • Overstated certainty. The five probe folds score the same images, so they are correlated views, not independent replications. There was no correction for testing many attribute–edit combinations. The intervals ignore uncertainty from probe fitting, from matching, and from using a single random seed.
  • No human review. No blinded dermatologist judged whether the edits looked realistic or actually removed the structure.
  • Unknown identities. Patient and lesion identity is incomplete, and it is unknown whether these images were part of the encoder's pretraining.

Why an invalidated audit is still worth reading

This project shows how "the code ran," "the records check out," "the result is trustworthy" and "a claim is allowed" can come apart. The two passes are tempting precisely because they look clean, and the two failures are real information about how fragile this workflow is. Keeping both, honestly labeled, guards against selective reporting in either direction. The audit also found an inconsistency: the program's core manuscript described the gate as requiring all four edit types, while the code required three of four. That discrepancy has been flagged for correction.

What a genuine test would need

  • A clean P04 in which evaluation data never touches model selection, with attributes taken only from that clean gate. There should be no assumption that the same four will return.
  • A newly sealed cohort with no overlap in records, lesion groups, patients or near-duplicates.
  • Fuller matching, a minimum match rate, paired random draws and a common image set.
  • A do-nothing control, a whole-lesion check validated in advance, and one realistic pixel edit as the primary test.
  • Correction for multiple comparisons, repeated seeds, and blinded dermatologist review of realism.
  • A portable evidence bundle, independent review, and authorized certification.

Until all of that exists, P05 stays what its label says: an aborted, invalidated audit that is carefully documented and makes no claim.

The question

When dermatologists examine a skin lesion through a dermoscope, they look for named visual patterns. This project focused on five of them: pigment network, negative network, streaks, globules and milia-like cysts. The public ISIC 2018 challenge (Task 2) labelled images for exactly these attributes.

MedSigLIP is a medically tuned image encoder associated with the MedGemma family. It turns each image into an embedding, a list of 1,152 numbers. You can picture every image as a point in a space with 1,152 dimensions. A popular idea in AI interpretability is that human concepts might correspond to directions in such a space, so that each named pattern has its own recognisable heading.

Project P06 asked three technical questions about these embeddings:

  1. If you estimate a pattern's direction several times on slightly different data, do you keep getting roughly the same direction?
  2. How do the directions for different patterns relate to one another?
  3. When two patterns occur together, does the average embedding behave like the sum of their separate effects, or is something extra left over?

These are questions about the internal geometry of a model. No patient, diagnosis or clinical decision is involved.

How the probe was built

The images. The study used 750 ISIC images, grouped into 641 "conservative components". A component is a cluster of images that might be near-duplicates or otherwise related. A component is never split between training and testing, so look-alike images cannot leak answers across the split. Patient and lesion identifiers were not available. The grouping therefore reduces known duplication but cannot guarantee that different components come from different people.

Finding a direction. For each pattern, the team trained a logistic probe: a simple classifier that weights the 1,152 numbers to separate images with the pattern from images without it. Once normalised, those weights form the pattern's direction. Training used nested cross-validation:

  • The data were cut into five outer parts, and each part was held out once for testing.
  • Inside each training portion, four further splits chose among seven regularisation settings.

This produces five direction estimates per pattern and 725 model fits per run.

Checking consistency. The five fold directions were compared in pairs using cosine similarity, where 1 means the same direction and 0 means unrelated. Five directions give ten pairs. Software flagged a pattern as "stable" if the median of those ten cosines reached 0.80. The whole procedure was repeated with three random seeds (17, 29 and 43), each of which reshuffles which components land in which fold.

Checking combinations. For each of the ten pairs of patterns, images fall into four cells: both patterns, only the first, only the second, or neither. A pair was analysed only if every cell held at least 50 component groups. For qualifying pairs, the team averaged the embeddings in each cell and combined the four averages as a "difference of differences". The length of what is left over indicates how far the pair departs from simple addition.

The overall gate. The software reduced all of this to one yes/no answer. It returned "yes" only if at least two directions were flagged stable and at least three pairs could be analysed.

Stress-Testing a Skin-Imaging AI: How One Feature Readout Shifted Under Synthetic Photo Changes

Project P07 · Evidence status: EXPLORATORY_SENSITIVITY

The question

Dermoscopy photographs of skin lesions are rarely uniform. They get compressed for storage. Some are slightly out of focus, some are brighter or darker depending on the device, and many are framed by a black border or darkened corners. A dermatologist mostly looks past these quirks. Does an AI model do the same?

Project P07 examined one narrow version of that question. MedSigLIP is a medically tuned image encoder from the MedGemma family. It converts each image into a list of 1,152 numbers called an embedding. Earlier in the same research program, project P03 trained simple linear “probes” that read those numbers and produce a score for whether expert annotators marked a pigment network, the net-like brown pattern seen in many pigmented lesions. P07 changed only how each image looked, not the lesion's annotation, and asked whether the probe score moved.

Evidence status: what this work can and cannot support

Four questions are easy to confuse here, so it helps to take them one at a time.

The label EXPLORATORY_SENSITIVITY means what it says. The work maps where one representation is sensitive, for one fixed set of synthetic changes applied to one sample. It does not show that real-world artifacts change diagnoses, that any tested change is clinically realistic, that the findings hold for new patients, or that the model relies on borders as a shortcut. The sample also came from the same pool of images that had earlier been used to evaluate the probe, so it cannot serve as an independent confirmation. An early automated field reading “eligible for report” meant only that the annotation labels were complete. It was never a publication decision. The rest of this article describes the work within those limits.

How the study was designed

The starting pool was 1,895 images from the public ISIC 2018 challenge, a single data source, all in a development partition. A fixed random seed (17) selected 500 of them. The selection took at most one image per encoded group, a partial stand-in for “same patient or same lesion,” because full identity information does not exist. It also balanced the sample at 250 images without a pigment-network annotation and 250 with one. That balance was a design choice and says nothing about how common the feature is.

Each image was then altered with five families of transformation, at 22 settings in total. The changes were applied to the original pixels before the encoder's usual resizing to a 448-pixel view.

These numbers are software settings, not calibrated severities. The images came in 174 different width-and-height combinations, so the same blur setting blurs a small image relatively more than a large one. A border of 10% per side can remove far more than 10% of the picture and may cover part of an off-centre lesion. Heavy brightening can clip colours. No dermatologist rated whether the changes look realistic before the scores were examined.

What was measured

P03 did not produce one probe but five, each trained on a different overlapping slice of the data (“folds”). P07 applied all five separately. For every image, fold and setting, the run recorded the probe's raw score (a logit) before and after the change, the signed difference, and how far the embedding itself moved. That gives 500 × 5 × 22 = 55,000 rows.

To get one number per image, the reviewers averaged the five fold-specific changes and then summarised across the 250 images in each annotation group. This averaging was done after the run and was not part of it. The five probes may use different score scales, so the average is a convenient summary, not a single calibrated measurement.

What the numbers showed

Black borders caused the largest shifts, raising scores in both groups. Across the 2%, 5% and 10% settings, the averages were +0.403, +1.140 and +1.712 without the annotation and +0.456, +1.136 and +1.672 with it. Blur lowered scores more as it strengthened: −0.021, −0.081, −0.159 and −0.357 without the annotation, and −0.022, −0.094, −0.303 and −0.747 with it. Vignetting behaved similarly, at −0.260, −0.484 and −0.702 versus −0.314, −0.559 and −0.779. Brightness and compression showed no simple pattern. These sequences are observations, not tested trends.

Averages can hide disagreement. At JPEG quality 30, the group averages point in opposite directions, but the individual probes do not agree:

Some reports treat a logit of zero as a yes/no line. Here that line was not meaningful even before any change: 1,148 of 1,250 fold readings (91.84%) in the group without the annotation were already at or above zero, compared with 1,249 of 1,250 in the annotated group. With a 10% border, 80 of 1,250 readings in the unannotated group crossed zero. They came from 40 images with at least one crossing, 13 where most folds crossed, and 2 where all five did. The annotated group had 1 crossing in 1,250. These crossings are software telemetry. They are not diagnostic errors.

How the record changed over time

Limitations

  • The study used one public source, one random sample and one annotated feature. Patient and lesion identity are incomplete.
  • The images had already been used to evaluate the probe, so this is not independent confirmation.
  • The transformations are synthetic, and their strengths are not comparable across images or families.
  • Nothing distinguishes why borders matter: covering the lesion, padding effects, and overall brightness shifts all remain possible.
  • The fold-averaged summary was added after the run, and no linked uncertainty estimates exist.
  • It is unknown whether these specific images were in the encoder's original training data.
  • No diagnosis, accuracy, safety or patient outcome was measured.

Why it still matters

A negative or tightly limited result can still be a useful audit. P07 shows that at least one readout from a medical image encoder is not indifferent to how an image is presented, and that a black frame can move its score substantially. That makes border handling, blur normalisation and fold-by-fold reporting clear priorities for any future test. The project also shows why tidy averages need checking: the apparent reversal under compression mostly disappears when each probe is examined on its own. Any future study that settles these questions would need new, independent images, transformations whose realism is reviewed before any scores are seen, and a summary measure fixed in advance.

Can an Image Model Be Made to Forget Where a Skin Photo Came From? An Audit of a Suppression Attempt That Did Not Succeed

Project status: MITIGATION_GATE_FAILED

This retrospective study used 1,998 dermoscopy images from four public datasets. It found that an image's dataset of origin was easy to read from its AI representation. A fixed linear procedure designed to suppress that information left it largely readable. A corrected rerun with three seeds reproduces exactly from its saved outputs, and it confirms that the fix did not work.

The question behind the project

Many AI tools for dermoscopy (magnified photographs of skin lesions) are built on a general-purpose image encoder. The encoder turns each photo into a long list of numbers called a representation. This project's title identifies the encoder as MedSigLIP. The concern is straightforward. Suppose those numbers carry a strong fingerprint of the collection a photo came from: its cameras, lighting, curation choices, patient mix or labeling habits. Then any tool built on top of the representation could learn shortcuts tied to the collection rather than the lesion.

One popular remedy is to find the directions in the representation that separate collections and cut them out, like muting the loudest instrument in a recording. Project P08 asked whether one specific version of that remedy works on these representations, and whether it disturbs diagnostic information along the way.

The short answer is no. After suppression, the dataset label was still easy to recover.

What was studied

The cohort is balanced by design: 500 images each from BCN20000, DERM12345 and HAM10000, plus 498 from HIBA. The first three are the "development" sources used to learn and remove dataset directions. HIBA was set aside entirely. It was never used to fit the suppression or train the probes, so it shows how a diagnosis probe behaves on a collection it has never seen.

Each image also carries a binary diagnosis label produced by a historical mapping rule. That rule has not been checked by dermatologists, so these are "operational" labels, not clinical ground truth. According to the 12 September check, no images had to be excluded for missing or non-binary labels. Two further cautions apply. Equal numbers per dataset do not reflect real-world case mixes. Patient identity is missing for two collections and lesion identity for a third, so some photos of the same patient or lesion could fall on both sides of a train/test split.

How the suppression procedure works

The method has three steps. First, a simple classifier (logistic regression) is trained to guess which development dataset an image came from. Its internal weights point along the directions that best distinguish the datasets. Second, those directions are projected out of the representation. Third, the process repeats. In the corrected version, directions from three fixed rounds are pooled and removed in one clean mathematical projection. New "probe" classifiers are then trained from scratch to check two things: can the dataset still be read, and can the diagnosis still be read?

Several safeguards limit self-deception. Images were split into five outer folds that keep known related images together. The whole analysis was repeated with three fixed random seeds (17, 29 and 43), and all three are reported rather than the best one. The held-out test fold and HIBA were always excluded from fitting. Nothing was stopped or tuned based on test results. Probe settings were fixed (regularization C = 1, up to 2,000 iterations), and every transform and classifier parameter was saved.

Status: HISTORICAL_EXPLORATORY_CORRECTED_RUN_ABSENT

The short version

Project P27 asked whether a computer model could look at the photos in a case from SCIN, a public dermatology image collection, and offer a short list of likely condition families. It was also allowed to hold back on the cases it was least sure about. The success measure was simple: among the cases the model chose to answer, how often was the right family somewhere in its top three guesses?

A run on 28 August 2026 reported 0.849, and the project log marked it as passing. The current configuration sets the bar at 0.8. A few days later, an internal audit found that the rule for deciding which cases to answer had been set by looking at the very cases being scored. A separate problem remains open: the grouping of individual diagnoses into families was a draft that nobody had reviewed. The code was repaired on 2 September. As of the 12 September review, no corrected run had been carried out. The 0.849 figure therefore belongs to the project's history, not to its findings.

What the project was trying to do

When a dermatologist sees an unfamiliar rash, the first step is usually a differential: a ranked list of plausible explanations. P27 tried to do a coarse version of this. Instead of naming a single disease, the model ranks ten broad families: eczema-like (eczematous), scaly-plaque (papulosquamous), infectious, acne and hair-follicle (acneiform/follicular), hive-like (urticarial), pigment-related, nail, growths (neoplastic), blood-vessel (vascular), and "other."

The second idea is abstention. A useful assistant should know when to stay quiet. The design targeted answering about 80% of cases and deferring the least confident 20%. The question was whether the answered cases were handled well enough to clear the bar.

How the experiment was built

The pipeline has several stages. A frozen image model (MedSigLIP, according to the configuration) turns each photo into a numerical summary, or "feature." Two classifiers were trained on those features. One is a simple logistic baseline that averages the features across a case's photos. The other is a "set encoder" that reads all of a case's photos together. Cases were divided into separate groups (splits), each with its own job:

  • TRAIN: teaches the classifiers.
  • DEV: picks the better classifier by how well its probabilities fit the truth.
  • CAL: calibrates the chosen model so its confidence scores mean what they say, and builds "conformal" prediction sets. In the corrected code, CAL also sets the abstention cutoff.
  • Evaluation: scores the final result.

Labels come from each case's leading diagnosis, which is then mapped to one of the ten families. A family counts only if it has at least 10 training cases, and the run stops if fewer than 10 families qualify. Cases that fall outside the supported families are dropped, not lumped into a leftover bucket.

There are two separate ideas of "not answering" in the code, and they are easy to confuse. The headline endpoint uses a confidence cutoff: keep a case only if the model's top probability is at or above a threshold. The code also builds conformal prediction sets at an error level of 0.10, capped at 5 families. In the saved prediction file, a case is flagged "abstain" when its set reaches that cap. The headline number uses the cutoff, not that flag.

Why the historical number cannot stand as a result

The cutoff was set by looking at the test

Think of a teacher who decides, after seeing every student's paper, to grade only the 80% who seemed most confident. The teacher never looked at the answers themselves. But the rule was built around this particular class, so it guarantees exactly 80% coverage here and says little about the next class. A real tool has to fix its cutoff in advance and then find out how many new cases clear it. In the historical run, the cutoff came from the confidence scores of the evaluation cases. The model did not see their correct labels, but the rule was still fitted to the group being scored. The current code keeps that old calculation, labeled as descriptive, so the history can be traced.

How much this inflated 0.849, if at all, is unknown. The effect might be small. It has not been measured, and the honest position is that the number describes a rule nobody could have used in practice.

The answer key itself was a draft

Every case's "right answer" is a family produced by a table that maps specific diagnoses to families. The configuration points to a file named as a candidate mapping, and the 12 September review states that this mapping has not been reviewed. If a diagnosis is filed under the wrong family, both the training targets and the scoring change. No amount of rerunning fixes that until someone qualified settles the mapping.

What the repair changes

In the corrected code, the cutoff is the 20th percentile of the calibrated top-probability scores on the CAL split. It is fitted there and then applied, unchanged, to the evaluation cases. The code refuses to run if anyone points the cutoff at the evaluation split. The corrected report must also state the realized coverage, meaning how many evaluation cases actually cleared the fixed cutoff, since it will no longer be exactly 80%. It must give the number of cases kept and, for each family, how many cases there were and how often the model was right. The success bar in the current configuration is still a top-3 recall of at least 0.8.

All of this is written and tested as software. None of it has been run on the real data.

Other limits worth knowing

Why this still matters

Letting a model say "I'm not sure" is one of the more promising ways to make image-based tools safer. The value of abstention, though, depends on a rule that is fixed before new cases arrive. This audit shows how easily a reasonable-looking number can rest on a rule that could never be used in advance, and why the grouping that defines the right answer needs review as careful as the model itself. Catching both problems before publication is a completed and useful piece of work, even though it leaves no validated result behind.

What would settle it

For P27 to become a reportable SCIN benchmark, three things are needed. First, a qualified reviewer settles the diagnosis-to-family mapping. Second, the corrected pipeline runs with its fixed cutoff. Third, the report gives realized coverage and per-family counts next to the headline recall, and it is clear that the evaluation cases were already exposed. Even then, the result would describe benchmark performance on a public dataset. It would not show that the model can safely triage real patients.

An experiment asked whether a model that sees a skin photo together with a person's own answers about their symptoms would do better than a model that sees only one of them. In the single run that was completed, it did not.

Evidence status: HISTORICAL_EXPLORATORY_NO_FUSION_BENEFIT

The question

A photo sent to a remote skin service usually comes with answers to a questionnaire: how long the problem has lasted, whether it itches or hurts, how it feels to the touch, and where on the body it is. Many forms also ask for age group, sex and skin type. You would expect a computer model given the picture and the answers to beat one given the picture alone.

Project P28 tested that expectation directly. It used SCIN, a public collection of skin-condition cases that pair photographs with answers people reported themselves. The question was whether a model combining the image with those answers would predict a case's broad diagnostic family more accurately than the best model working from a single source. The one completed run answered no.

How the experiment was built

A SCIN case can contain several photographs. The pipeline turned each photo into a list of numbers using a pre-trained medical image encoder called MedSigLIP, then averaged those lists so every case had one image summary. The encoder was not retrained. The experiment only learned how to read what it produced.

Two groups of questionnaire answers sat alongside the image summary. The symptom group covered how long the condition had lasted, its texture, the body sites involved and the listed symptoms. The demographic group covered age group, sex at birth, Fitzpatrick skin type and self-reported race or ethnicity. The model had to name one of ten broad families: eczematous, papulosquamous, infectious, acneiform/follicular, urticarial, pigmentary, nail, neoplastic, vascular or other.

Six models were fitted on the training cases. Each was a logistic regression, a simple and well-understood classifier, with inputs rescaled and classes weighted to offset imbalance. They differed only in what they were allowed to see.

Where the result could mislead

The model that had to win used all three sources, not photo plus symptoms

The project is described as "image plus symptoms". The model that had to win, though, was the all-three model: photo, symptoms and demographics together. It was compared only with photo-only and symptoms-only. The photo-plus-symptoms model was fitted but was not the headline. So the historical result says the everything-combined model lost. It does not tell us how photo plus symptoms did on its own, and any write-up should report that model separately.

Only one, fairly crude, way of combining was tried

The combination was "early fusion". The long image summary and the short list of answers were placed side by side and given to one classifier with a single shared penalty on its weights. That recipe is easy to check but crude. The image contributes far more numbers than the questionnaire, and one penalty may not treat the two kinds of information fairly. A loss here is evidence against this recipe on this data. It is not evidence that symptoms carry no diagnostic information.

There are no error bars, and repeating the run with new seeds cannot supply them

A logistic regression fitted this way gives the same answer every time. The plan listed three random seeds (17, 29 and 43), and its settings file still names spread across seeds as part of the uncertainty method. With fits that give the same answer every time, the three seeds simply reproduce one number. The revised code accepts this. It resamples cases 2,000 times (a bootstrap, with seed 17) to put an interval around the gain instead. Because that version has not been run, the historical −0.292 has no interval. We know the point estimate was negative, but not how far from zero it plausibly lies. The settings text should also be corrected so that it no longer suggests seeds contribute uncertainty.

The blank-out tests do not simulate people skipping questions

The stress tests replaced symptom or demographic inputs with zeros before the model rescaled them, and the model had been trained only on complete answers. After rescaling, a zero is not a neutral "unknown". It is a specific and often unusual value that the model never saw next to a real photo. The random variant blanks 30 percent of individual entries, not whole unanswered questions. These are perturbation checks, not realistic missing-answer tests, and the later review recommends dropping any missing-data interpretation unless that analysis is corrected and recomputed.

Labels, people and prior exposure

The ten families come from a candidate file that maps SCIN's detailed labels into broad groups. A sibling project that uses the same file notes that this mapping has not been reviewed. If the mapping changes, so does what every model is being asked to predict. SCIN cases are grouped by case, and no patient identity links submissions, so one person could in principle have cases in both the training set and the evaluation set. The symptom answers are self-reported. Nobody knows whether the image encoder saw SCIN photos during its own pre-training. Finally, the packet does not show exactly which held-out split produced the headline gain, so its separation from the development check cannot be confirmed here. None of these points explains the negative result specifically, but each one limits how far any SCIN number can be generalized.

What the result does and does not show

A fair summary: in one completed exploratory run on SCIN, a simple early-fusion logistic regression using photo, symptom and demographic inputs did not reach a lower log loss than the better of the photo-only and symptoms-only models.

It does not show that symptoms are useless for recognizing skin conditions, that photo-only models are good enough to sort patients, or that combining information never helps. It does not show that the model copes with missing answers, and it supports no clinical decision about any individual.

What would strengthen or change the picture

  • Run the revised code once, tied to the exact data and settings, to get the gain with its case-level interval.
  • Settle the diagnostic-family mapping first, so every model predicts a reviewed target.
  • Report photo plus symptoms as its own comparison, alongside the all-three model.
  • Either drop the blank-out tests from interpretation or redesign them, for example by training with deliberately hidden answers and an explicit "not answered" flag.
  • Optionally, try a different way of combining, such as fitting the photo and questionnaire models separately and then merging their probabilities. The review treats a new fusion model as optional, not required.

Why a negative result is worth keeping

Intake questionnaires cost patients time, and software builders often assume that more inputs mean a smarter model. P28 is a documented case where that assumption failed under a simple, transparent design: adding structured answers to a strong image summary did not help. Stated plainly and with its limits attached, it can sit in a SCIN paper as a bounded comparison that stops readers from assuming a fusion benefit that was never shown. That makes it a finished and useful audit, even though it is not a medical finding.

The question behind the project

When a dermatologist looks at a rash, the answer is usually a ranked shortlist: "most likely eczema, possibly psoriasis, less likely a fungal infection." Most image classifiers, though, are trained as if every case had exactly one correct label. Everything except the top pick is thrown away.

P29 tests whether keeping the whole shortlist helps. If a model learns how much weight experts gave each possibility, its own output might track that expert spread more closely. On a truly ambiguous case it would hedge where experts hedge, instead of committing confidently to one answer. This is a question about agreement with a reference opinion. It does not test diagnostic accuracy, and it says nothing about patient benefit.

The data: shortlists, not single answers

The project uses SCIN, a public dermatology image collection in which each case carries a weighted differential diagnosis. The project's own code warns that these weights reflect a consensus derived from how dermatologists ranked the options. They are not calibrated probabilities of disease. A model that matches them is matching expert ranking behaviour, not the true odds.

Specific diagnoses are grouped into ten broad families: eczematous, papulosquamous, infectious, acneiform/follicular, urticarial, pigmentary, nail, neoplastic, vascular and other. The table that maps diagnoses to families is still labelled a candidate and awaits review. Each case's images become one numerical summary by averaging features from a pretrained medical image encoder (MedSigLIP). Only a small classifier layered on top of those features is trained. Data splits keep each case's images together, grouped by case rather than by patient.

Design: three ways to learn from the same cases

Three classifiers are trained on identical inputs. They differ only in what they are asked to reproduce.

How success is scored

Two yardsticks compare a model's output with the experts' weighted shortlist:

  • Jensen–Shannon divergence measures how far apart two sets of weights are. Lower is better; zero means identical.
  • nDCG@5 scores how well the model's top five families follow the experts' order. Higher is better.

For each seed, the better of the two soft-label variants (judged by divergence) is compared with the hard-label model. The seed counts as a success only if the soft model wins on both yardsticks. Under the current rule the project succeeds if at least two of the three seeds succeed.

Why reusing the tuning split matters

Picture a student who uses one practice exam to decide when to stop studying, uses the same exam to decide which of two study methods worked better, and is then graded on that exam. A high score is encouraging. It is not a fair measure of what the student will do on an unseen test, because every decision was made to look good on those particular questions.

The August run had the same structure. Each model was stopped at the moment its divergence on DEV looked best. The better soft variant was then chosen on DEV. Finally, DEV decided whether that chosen model beat the hard-label model. Every step fits a little to the quirks of that one group of cases, and the soft side gets an extra advantage because it is the best of two tries while the comparator gets one.

None of this shows that soft labels do not help. The effect may well be real. The August test simply cannot separate a genuine benefit from the optimism that repeated selection builds in.

The question

Dermatologists often look at a suspicious spot in more than one way. An ordinary photograph, taken close up or from farther away, shows the lesion’s outline, color and surroundings. A dermatoscope is a handheld magnifier with controlled lighting, and it shows pigment patterns and structures that the naked eye misses. Project P30 asked a narrow, practical version of an old question: if a computer model already has the most informative single kind of image, does giving it the other kind as well improve its benign-versus-malignant judgment?

The images came from MIDAS, a public collection in which many lesions were photographed clinically and through a dermatoscope. The project’s design notes describe the target cohort as complete, biopsied lesions. Because the same lesion appears in both kinds of image, the question can in principle be answered cleanly: score the same lesions with one view, then with the other, then with both.

How the study was built

No neural network was trained from scratch. Each image was converted into a list of numbers (a “feature vector”) by a pre-trained medical image encoder that the configuration names as MedSigLIP, and the encoder itself was left unchanged. Images were sorted into three views: close clinical, distant clinical and dermoscopic. Where a lesion had several images of one view, their features were averaged. A lesion counted as “complete” only if it had all three views.

Simple logistic-regression models were then trained on these features. There were nine combinations: patient metadata alone (age, sex, body site, skin type, lesion diameter), each image view alone, both clinical views together, clinical plus dermoscopic, and mixtures of images with metadata. The data were split into three parts. A training portion fitted the models. A development portion decided which single image source was strongest. A held-out evaluation portion was used for the headline comparison between that best single source and the clinical-plus-dermoscopic pair.

The score was negative log-likelihood. In plain terms, it measures how confidently a model put probability on the correct answer, with lower values being better. The main result was the difference: single-source score minus paired score. A positive difference would mean that adding the second kind of image helped, and the registered success criterion was simply “greater than zero.”

The design also included caution in advance. If the held-out portion had fewer than 150 complete lesions or fewer than 30 malignant cases, any result would automatically be treated as descriptive only. If the whole complete cohort had fewer than 20 lesions, the run would stop.

An exploratory test in August 2026 found that a learned projection helped match clinical photos to dermoscopic images of the same lesion. The set of candidate images used for scoring was later found to be flawed. The code has been fixed, but no new results have been produced.

The question in plain terms

When a dermatologist examines a spot on the skin, they often photograph it twice. The first picture is an ordinary clinical photograph showing the lesion and some surrounding skin. The second is a dermoscopic image, taken through a handheld magnifier with controlled lighting, which shows pigment patterns and structures beneath the surface. The two pictures look very different, but they show the same lesion.

Project P31 asked whether a computer can recognize that the two pictures belong together. Given a clinical photo, can it pick the dermoscopic image of the same lesion out of a lineup of candidates? Can it do the same in reverse? This task is called cross-modal retrieval. The project used MIDAS, a public dataset that has both kinds of image for many lesions.

The test shows something about what an image model has learned. If the model can link two very different views of one lesion, it is probably capturing features of the lesion rather than of the camera. Passing the test is still a narrow technical result. It says nothing about diagnosis, and the project did not measure clinical usefulness.

How the experiment was built

The experiment started from MedSigLIP, a pretrained medical image model. The model turns each image into an embedding, a long list of numbers that summarizes the image. The model was kept frozen, meaning it was not retrained. When a lesion had several photos of the same type, their embeddings were averaged. Each lesion therefore ended up with one clinical summary and one dermoscopic summary.

Two approaches were compared:

  • Baseline: compare the frozen embeddings directly and measure how similar each clinical summary is to each dermoscopic one.
  • Challenger: add a small learned “projection,” a layer trained to bring each lesion's two views closer together and push views of different lesions apart. This contrastive training used only the training lesions. A separate development set decided when to stop training.

How success would be measured was set before the run. For each query image, the system ranks every candidate in the gallery. Recall at 1 is the fraction of queries where the correct partner is ranked first. It was computed in both directions (clinical to dermoscopic, and dermoscopic to clinical) and the two were averaged. The projection counted as a success only if it raised this average by at least 0.05 over the baseline. The plan also listed per-direction scores, recall at 5 and mean reciprocal rank as metrics to report. It did not allow training to use patient identity or any hints about held-out pairs.

A skeptical reading

Five issues limit what the historical number can mean.

The lineup was not clean

Retrieval is scored against a gallery of candidates. If any candidate other than the true partner is ranked first, the query counts as a miss. MIDAS contains near-duplicate images filed under different lesion records, as well as related lesions. Under the original scoring, if the system picked a near-copy of the correct image that was filed under another lesion, the query was counted as a miss. Entries like these can distort both the baseline and the projection. Removing them could make the gap between the two either larger or smaller.

Patients are unknown

The dataset version used here has no patient identifiers. The code handles this correctly. It marks the harder “same-patient” test, where the lineup contains only lesions from one person, as unverifiable rather than inventing a result. The missing identifiers also mean that nobody can confirm the same person does not appear in both the training and evaluation data, or tell which gallery negatives are other lesions from the same patient.

Twenty-eight pairs is small

In a gallery this small, each hit or miss changes the average noticeably. The only planned uncertainty measure is the spread across seeds, which does not capture variation from sampling different lesions.

The development set did double duty

The development set decides when training stops, and it also decides the pass/fail gate. The code reports its headline result on a separate exploratory test split when one exists, and on the development set otherwise. The files do not say which case applied in the historical run. If the development set was used, the number is optimistic by construction.

Shortcuts are possible

The project's own caveat warns that retrieval may rely on acquisition shortcuts, meaning cues about how or where the images were taken, rather than on the lesion itself. Nothing in this run tests for that.

The September 2 correction, and what it has not yet done

An audit on September 2 led to a code fix for the first problem. Now, when a gallery image is in the same near-duplicate group as the query but belongs to a different lesion, it is removed from that query's lineup. It counts as neither a hit nor a miss. The rule applies in both directions, and the number of hidden entries is recorded for each direction. The same rule is used when the development set picks the stopping point, so model selection and reporting use the same gallery. The run was renamed p31-exploratory-20260902-v2 so its results cannot be confused with the original.

After the fix, the repository's automated test suite passed (369 tests). This shows the code behaves as its tests expect. It is not a new scientific result. The log records that no cluster rerun was done because the VPN connection needed to reach the cluster was down. The September 12 review confirmed that the gallery changes had produced no new run outputs.

The fix itself has two limits. First, it hides only images flagged as belonging to the same near-duplicate group. Related but distinct lesions from one patient can still appear in the lineup as negatives. Second, a comment in the configuration file says behavior is unchanged when no external duplicate list is supplied. In fact, the code falls back to a duplicate column in the data manifest when that column exists. The comment and the code should be brought into line before a rerun, so readers know which gallery definition was used.

  • No claims about same-patient performance or broad generalization while patient identifiers are unavailable.

The September 12 review makes a modest recommendation. P31 should be kept as a supplement to MIDAS work only if the per-direction retrieval and the corrected gallery definition can be supported.

Why this is still worth reading

A promising first number followed by the discovery of a flaw in the lineup is not a failure. The project set its success threshold before running and compared against a sensible baseline. It refused to invent a patient-level result. When auditors noticed that near-duplicate images could make correct answers count as mistakes, the team fixed the scoring and renamed the run instead of overwriting the old result. What is left is a clear, testable question with a known next step. Until the rerun exists, the accurate summary is: an early signal from a small sample, with the corrected method not yet tested.

Does a photo add anything? A corrected test of combining smartphone skin images with patient information

Project P32 · PAD-UFES-20 smartphone photographs · Status label: CORRECTED_EXPLORATORY_CLAIM_USE_NONE

The question

Suppose someone photographs a worrying spot on their skin with a phone. A computer can then draw on two kinds of information. One is the picture. The other is a short list of facts: the person's age and sex, where the spot is on the body, how large it is, and whether it itches, has grown, hurts or has changed. It seems obvious that combining the two, an approach called fusion, should beat either one alone. Project P32 set out to test that idea fairly using PAD-UFES-20, a public research collection of smartphone photographs gathered in Brazil.

After a repaired analysis, the answer is not the one the idea predicts. Combining a fixed set of image features with patient information clearly beat the image features alone, but it did not reliably beat the patient information alone. The study's preset bar for success was not cleared.

Evidence status: what this result is and is not

The status label is CORRECTED_EXPLORATORY_CLAIM_USE_NONE. This is an exploratory reanalysis of a dataset that had already been used. Its result certificate records it as not certified, and it authorizes no scientific or clinical claim. When software runs, that does not mean its answer is trustworthy. A trustworthy answer, in turn, is not automatically permission to make a claim. The table keeps these questions separate.

How the comparison was designed

The collection holds 2,298 photographs from 1,373 recorded patients. Each photograph was labelled cancer (basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma or melanoma) or non-cancer (actinic keratosis, nevus or seborrheic keratosis). This is a benchmark grouping. Lesions within each group would not all be managed the same way in a clinic.

Three simple models were compared, all of the same type (logistic regression) and all tested on the same splits:

  • Image only: a stored list of 1,152 numbers per photograph, produced earlier by an image model. The study configuration associates that model with MedSigLIP.
  • Metadata only: the patient and lesion facts listed above.
  • Fusion: both lists joined end to end.

Performance was measured with average precision (AP) for cancer. AP scores how well a model places cancers ahead of non-cancers when cases are ranked by predicted risk. A perfect ranking scores 1.0. The test was five-fold cross-validation. The development data were split into five parts, and each part took one turn as the test set while the other four were used for training. Before any results were seen, the team fixed the rule for success: fusion had to beat both single-source models in at least 4 of the 5 folds.

Two parts of the design deserve credit. First, fusion was compared with metadata as well as with images. Beating image features alone would not show that the photograph adds anything to what the metadata already carry. Second, the success rule stayed as written after the results came in.

What had to be fixed

Combining photographs. Some lesions were photographed more than once. The corrected analysis averages image features only across photos that share both the same patient and the same lesion identifier. Those photos also had to agree on diagnosis and data split. If they recorded conflicting facts, processing stopped. This produced 1,891 "record groups", which are working units rather than confirmed biological lesions.

Keeping related records together. If one person's photos appear in both training and testing, a model can look better than it really is. The corrected analysis linked records that shared a patient, a lesion identifier, an identical image file or an existing duplicate marker. Linked records form 1,130 "relationship components". Each component always stays entirely on one side of every split.

Reading symptoms. True/false answers are now converted to yes and no. "Unknown" stays separate from a recorded "no", and unexpected values stop the run.

A reviewer's reading

Three points deserve emphasis beyond the manuscript's own framing.

The tiny gap changes direction depending on how it is summarized. Pooling all predictions puts fusion ahead by about 0.0000084. Averaging the five fold scores instead puts metadata ahead: 0.924901 against 0.924841 for fusion. When the sign of a difference depends on the arithmetic used to summarize it, the fair statement is that no difference was demonstrated.

Some planned measures are missing. The analysis plan named two further measures. One was sensitivity when specificity is held at 90%, which shows how many cancers are caught when false alarms are kept low. The other was calibration error, which shows whether predicted risks match observed rates. Neither has verified values in the report. How these models would behave at a practical decision threshold is therefore unknown.

Some labels in the records are inconsistent. The stored metrics file describes the main endpoint as "patient-grouped", although the splits actually grouped the broader relationship components. The file also marks the endpoint as not eligible without saying why. Neither issue changes the numbers, but both should be cleaned up before anyone relies on these files.

Limitations

  • One reused dataset. There was no untouched external cohort and no test in a real clinical workflow.
  • Unverified image-feature origin. The stored features cannot be traced to an exact image-model version. Conclusions apply to this fixed set of features, not to MedSigLIP or to image models in general.
  • Working identities. Record groups and components are built from recorded identifiers. Nobody reviewed them by hand to confirm which images show the same lesion or near-duplicate photographs.
  • Weighting. Each record group counts once, so patients with several lesions carry more weight than patients with one.
  • Bundled repairs. The three fixes were made together, so this analysis cannot say which defect mattered most. It also says nothing about the value of any single symptom.
  • Not a triage tool. Nothing here shows safety, fairness across skin types or patient groups, or benefit to patient care.

Why this negative result still matters

The earlier version of this analysis produced numbers that could not be trusted. The corrected version produced numbers that can be traced from start to finish, and they did not support the hoped-for improvement. That is a real finding. In this dataset, with these simple models, a short list of patient and lesion facts did about as well as those facts plus stored image features. A comparison that left out the metadata-only model would have made fusion look better than it is.

The project also shows a practical method. The rules for averaging photographs together can differ from the rules for keeping related records apart, and splitting on patient alone can let related records leak between training and testing. Future studies of image-plus-metadata models would be stronger with fresh data, image features traced to an exact model version, hand-checked lesion identities, and the missing threshold and calibration measures. Until then, P32 is best read as a completed and carefully checked audit whose answer to the main question is "not shown".

When the Captions All Say the Same Thing

An audit of P42, a pilot that tried to align dermatology images with short text descriptions. The pilot passed its own test in August 2026. A September review found the pass was hollow.

The idea behind the pilot

Modern medical image models often come in pairs. One part turns a photograph into a list of numbers (an "embedding"). A second part does the same for a sentence. If the two parts are well aligned, a photo of a rash and a sentence describing that rash land close together in the same numerical space. That lets you search for images using words, or find the description that best fits a photo.

Project P42 asked a modest version of that question. It started with an existing medical image–text model, MedSigLIP, and kept it frozen. On top of it, the pilot trained a small, simple mapping layer (a "projection"). The goal was to test whether this layer could pull dermatology photos and their matching text closer together than the frozen model already did. The photos came from SCIN, a public collection of skin-condition images. The text was not free-form captions. It was sentences built from the structured information recorded with each case, such as diagnosis, body site, reported symptoms, texture and condition category. The original project plan described the target as "provenance-grounded image/text pairs" with a goal of "retrieval and concept gains". It budgeted four to eight GPUs for three to seven days.

This article explains what was built and what the August run showed. It also covers what was wrong with that run, what has been fixed, and why the project is now on hold.

Evidence status

The project carries the status label HISTORICAL_PILOT_DEGENERATE_TEXT_SIDE. In plain terms, a pilot run was completed, and its "pass" rests on text that had collapsed into a handful of repeated strings. The table below separates four questions that are easy to blur together.

How the pilot was built

The pipeline has two lanes that meet in the middle. In the image lane, each SCIN photo goes through the frozen MedSigLIP image encoder. In the text lane, the case's recorded fields are rendered into a short factual sentence. For example: "Body site: arm. Observed morphology: raised. Reported symptoms: itching. Condition category: rash." The sentence then goes through the matching frozen text encoder. Both lanes then feed a trainable linear projection. It is trained with a standard "contrastive" objective, which rewards each image for being closer to its own sentence than to anyone else's.

The corrected configuration trains for 30 epochs with a batch size of 256 and a 512-dimensional projection. It repeats the run across three random seeds (17, 29 and 43). Success needs two things together on the held-out evaluation data:

  • Retrieval: a better retrieval score than the frozen baseline. The score is the average of "recall at 10" in both directions, meaning how often the correct partner appears in the top ten matches.
  • Concepts: a better concept score than the baseline. The concept score measures whether retrieved items share clinical concepts, such as the same body site or symptom.

Uncertainty is reported as the lowest, middle and highest result across the three seeds. There is no population-level confidence interval.

What went wrong in August

The August run reported a pass. The problem was on the text side. According to the project log, all those images were paired with only 11 distinct text strings.

An analogy helps. Imagine a matching game where hundreds of photos must each be paired with their own caption card, but almost every card reads the same thing. When a photo is equally close to dozens of identical cards, which one counts as "correct" is decided by tiny numerical tie-breaks, not by anything the model understood. Exact-pair retrieval stops measuring alignment and starts measuring accidents of ordering. A small trained layer could shift those accidents enough to "beat" the baseline without learning anything meaningful about skin images and language.

That is why the September 12 review labels P42 "not a valid broad alignment result". It also advises against presenting the old gate as broad alignment success, and against rerunning with unchanged captions. The specific retrieval and concept numbers from August are not in the reviewed material, so this article does not report them.

Why a rerun would stop at the first step

The new diversity check counts how many of the sentences are distinct once a phrase describing the camera angle is set aside. The camera angle, called "shot type", is a fact about how the photo was taken, not about the skin. The pipeline requires that fraction to be at least 0.5. The project log reports that SCIN's development split, even with the corrected text builder, reaches only about 0.31. A rerun would therefore stop before training, which is exactly what the log predicts.

A code comment points to why this may be structural rather than fixable by tweaking. It says each SCIN case has three photos (close-up, at an angle, at a distance) whose sentences differ only by that camera-angle phrase. If that holds, three photos of one case share one clinical sentence. Even if every case had a unique description, distinctness counted per photo could not rise much above one-third. The observed 0.31 sits just under that ceiling. This is a reviewer's reading of the code comment, not a checked property of the dataset. Still, it implies that no amount of reprocessing SCIN's existing metadata will clear the bar. Genuinely richer, source-grounded text would be needed. It also implies the check, though a useful stop sign, mixes up two different situations: "several photos of one case" and "different cases with identical text". A future design should measure distinctness across cases.

Other limits a reader should know

  • The text contains the answer. The rendered sentences include the diagnosis and other case labels. Gains on concept matching could partly reflect the projection learning to sort by label, much like a classifier, rather than learning open-ended correspondence between images and language.
  • An easy baseline. The comparison is against the untrained, frozen model space. A layer trained on in-domain pairs would usually be expected to beat that, so a modest gain is weak evidence on its own.
  • Siblings still affect exact matching. The concept score now ignores other photos from the same case, but the exact-pair retrieval score does not. Near-identical sibling sentences can still muddy that measure.
  • Thin uncertainty. Three seeds give a sense of run-to-run wobble, not a confidence interval for the wider population of images.
  • No clinical meaning. Nothing here bears on diagnosis, triage or patient care. The pilot never set out to test clinical use, and it did not do so.

Why this audit is still worth having

A negative or hollow result sounds like wasted effort, but P42 shows the opposite. The failure was caught because the team looked at the inputs to its metric, not just the metric. Repetitive text is an easy trap in image–language work. Any dataset whose "captions" are really a small set of templated labels can produce impressive retrieval curves that mean very little. The corrected code turns that lesson into a guardrail. It checks text diversity before spending any compute, treats a missing value as a failure, and reports ties openly.

The project's own recommendation is sensible: do not rerun on the same captions, and do not cite the August pass as evidence of alignment. Revisit only if meaningful, source-grounded text becomes available, such as real clinical descriptions tied to each image. A successor study would also want a stronger in-domain comparator and a diversity measure that counts cases rather than photos. Until then, P42 stands as a completed audit that shows how a passing test can be empty, and how to build the check that exposes it.

What the program set out to test

"Retrieval-augmented generation" is a simple idea with an awkward name. Before an AI system answers, it looks up a few similar past examples and reads them next to the new case, much as a clinician might check a reference atlas. This program asked whether that helps vision-language models, meaning AI systems that read images and write text, to classify skin lesions.

The team started by adapting an unpublished mammography methods paper to dermatology. Their written protocol describes a large study across several hospitals, with pathology-confirmed outcomes, independent expert readers and safety thresholds fixed in advance. None of that has been carried out. What actually ran was a series of smaller experiments on Derm7pt, a public research dataset of 1,011 lesion cases. Each case has an ordinary clinical photograph and a dermoscopic image, which is a magnified, specially lit close-up. How the experiments were built

The pipeline has a few parts. First, an image encoder turns each case's pair of photographs into a numeric fingerprint. Two public encoders were tried, general-purpose OpenCLIP and BiomedCLIP, which was trained on biomedical figures. Each was used either unchanged or fine-tuned three separate times. For a new case, the system finds the most similar training cases and nothing else. In the text-based experiments it passes along five of their written reports. These reports describe visible features from a standard seven-point dermoscopy checklist but leave out the diagnosis, so the model can't simply copy an answer.

A frozen AI "reader" then gets the images, any retrieved material and an instruction. Qwen2.5-VL-7B was the main reader, and MedGemma-1.5-4B was run as a separate arm. Each case received one deterministic answer in a structured format: one of five diagnoses, or null. A strict parser checked every reply.

Two measures matter. Coverage is the share of all cases that got one usable diagnosis; refusals and garbled replies stay in the count. Balanced accuracy averages the hit rate across the five diagnostic categories. Nevi (moles) make up nearly half the cases, so a system that always says "nevus" scores perfectly on nevi and zero everywhere else, giving 0.200. That is the floor a useful model has to beat.

Chapter one: better search, no better diagnosis

In the first pilot, fine-tuning clearly improved search. The share of the five nearest retrieved cases that had the same diagnosis rose from 0.555 to between 0.692 and 0.716 for BiomedCLIP, and from 0.570 to between 0.713 and 0.733 for OpenCLIP. That improvement never reached diagnosis. With no retrieval, the Qwen reader declined every case: balanced accuracy 0.0, with 100% abstention. With nearest-neighbor retrieval, balanced accuracy was only 0.006 to 0.018, with 97% to 99% abstention. Even an "oracle" condition built as an upper bound reached just 0.053.

Two cautions apply. Unlike the later work, this pilot was scored on the dataset's test portion. And a September audit found that the pilot's instructions showed the model internal condition labels, which muddies its random, shuffled and oracle comparisons. The packet records no fix for that problem.

Chapter two: one phrase decided whether the model answered

Nearly total refusal can look like inability. But the instructions had offered "or null to abstain." A first follow-up removed that option. Qwen then answered every case, but badly: balanced accuracy 0.2507 and heavily skewed toward melanoma. MedGemma appeared to fail altogether. It later turned out that under one prompt, 137 of 203 replies ran into a 512-token length cap while the model wrote out a hidden reasoning preamble. A rerun with 2,048 tokens and a pre-filled answer opening produced readable output in every condition where the reasoning preamble was suppressed, and forced-choice balanced accuracy reached 0.380. Much of the apparent failure was a formatting problem.

The main experiment then compared two prompts that differed only by the words "or null to abstain." For Qwen, coverage went from 0 of 203 cases to 203 of 203 without retrieval, and from 4 to 203 with retrieval. No case moved the other way. A third, "calibrated" prompt allowed null only for unreadable images, and it covered just 1 and 26 cases. When the model's internal scores were probed on 50 sampled permissive cases, all 50 favored "null" over every diagnosis. Given permission, declining was a real preference, not a near tie.

Answering is not the same as being right. Forced-choice balanced accuracy was 0.267 without retrieval and 0.255 with it, against the 0.200 floor. Qwen called melanoma in 162 and 171 of 203 cases. It caught 56 of 61 melanomas but only 13 of 100 nevi without retrieval (7 with retrieval), and none of the seborrheic keratoses or miscellaneous lesions. The lower end of the recalculated interval for the no-retrieval excess over the floor was +0.0036, which is barely above zero. The authors explicitly do not claim that retrieval helps or that it has no effect.

MedGemma, analyzed separately because it received a different answer setup, covered 26 and 140 cases under the permissive prompt and all 203 when forced. It is not ranked against Qwen. The stored numbers were reproduced exactly, and a September replay regenerated every raw answer word for word. That shows the text is reproducible. It does not show the answers are correct.

Why it matters

Studies often treat an AI's refusal to answer as proof that it can't do a task, or treat a forced answer as proof that it can. This program shows how fragile both readings are.
