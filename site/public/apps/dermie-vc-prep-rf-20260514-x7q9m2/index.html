dermie. The pitch frame, the assets, the answers.

A working briefing for a venture capital meeting. Built to be read in twenty minutes and held in hand during the conversation that follows.

For Ramie Fathy, MD Re dermie / AI-native medical learning OS Read time ~18 min
01

The Frame

Don't pitch a board-prep app. Pitch the learning OS that begins with one.

What you actually have is significantly bigger than "dermie is a dermatology board-prep platform." You have three commercializable assets bundled inside one architecture, each with a different revenue model and a different exit story. Sequencing that for a VC matters more than detailing any single component.

The narrative arc that earns the check is to position dermie as the first vertical of an AI-native medical learning operating system, with dermatology as the beachhead because that is where you are clinically embedded, AAD-credentialed, and personally motivated. Dominate that wedge inside eighteen months; the same engine, knowledge graph, and content-generation pipeline extends to every other specialty, then to nursing, PA, dental, and eventually to any visual-and-reasoning-intensive learning domain.

The reason this frame works is that dermatology alone is a tiny venture market — roughly 1,700 US derm residents per year. Any sophisticated VC will pattern-match dermie-as-a-board-prep-app to a niche SaaS deal that won't return their fund. The platform-with-beachhead frame is what makes this a venture-scale story while remaining credible — because you actually have the components.

The frame that earns the meeting

Don't oversell the moonshot. Lead with what is shipping, then sketch the architectural inevitability of the expansion. VCs reward execution discipline plus a believable long arc; they punish either alone.

02

The Portfolio

Three commercializable assets, one architecture.

Each asset is a separate revenue path with a separate exit story. The strategic value of the bundle is that they compound each other — and that no single competitor has all three.

i

dermie — the AI-native adaptive learning platform

A production-ready board-preparation platform with personalization, calibration capture, and skin-tone-inclusive imagery. Direct B2C and B2B2C revenue. Competes with UWorld, Amboss, Sketchy, Boards & Beyond, and Truelearn but is the only AI-native platform in the category, not a static bank with AI bolted on.

The wedge is dermatology because that is where you can dominate within eighteen months. The architecture is specialty-agnostic from day one.

Revenue path B2C subscription · program license · institutional enterprise
ii

The evidence-backed dermatology knowledge graph (KnowDerm)

A separate-from-dermie asset with standalone licensing value to medical publishers, EMR vendors, clinical decision support platforms, CME providers, and patient-education companies. Also the grounding substrate that gives dermie content quality no AI-native competitor can match without years of curation work.

This is your moat against UWorld bolting AI onto their static bank — they do not have the substrate, and building it costs years.

Revenue path API licensing · enterprise data · grounding-as-a-service
iii

The Sketchy-style AI visual mnemonic generation pipeline

Potentially the single most valuable asset in the portfolio. Sketchy itself sold to Turnitin/TLM Holdings in 2021 for a deal reportedly valued in the hundreds of millions; their entire moat was hand-illustrated visual mnemonics with creative narrative scenes. You have replicated that quality with image-generation models — meaning you can produce content at one to three orders of magnitude lower cost basis.

This can be a standalone product, a feature inside dermie, or white-label licensed to other medical education companies and specialty societies.

Revenue path standalone product · platform feature · white-label license

04

The Moat

Four compounding advantages. They will ask, repeatedly.

Sophisticated investors will challenge the moat inside the first fifteen minutes. The honest answer is that defensibility lives in the intersection of four advantages, none of which a pure-tech competitor can shortcut.

Moat 01

The clinical content flywheel

Every user interaction generates feedback that improves the next generation of content, the calibration of the adaptive engine, and the targeting of the knowledge graph. UWorld has twenty years of static content. You accumulate dynamic content advantage from day one. Quantify it for the meeting: by month six with 500 active users, expect X reviewer-flagged items, Y calibration data points, Z gap-coverage signals.

Moat 02

The knowledge graph as substrate

Structurally hard to replicate because the curation cost is real, even with AI assistance. Competitors hit the same bottleneck you did — the human dermatologist time required to validate edges. Your AAD committee positioning is part of this moat: you have access to clinical experts that pure-tech competitors don't.

Moat 03

Clinical trust as distribution

The AAD Derm Tank pitch, the publication strategy, the resident network you are recruiting, your incoming Harvard fellowship — these compound into clinical-community trust that pure-tech competitors cannot manufacture on a venture timeline. UWorld has no clinician story; Amboss has clinician-built credibility but no AI-native foundation; Glass Health has clinical credibility but no education focus.

Moat 04

Architecture-as-moat

dermie is built specialty-agnostic from the start. This is a fundamental architectural decision that competitors cannot retrofit without rebuilding. The first specialty is a slog; the second is faster; the third is automatic. The compounding advantage is in the abstraction layers, not the content.

05

Anticipated Questions

Eleven questions. Honest, prepared answers.

Treat these as the script you have rehearsed but never read from. The answer matters less than the speed and confidence with which you give it. Practice each out loud.

01

"What's the TAM?"

Don't lead with derm-only numbers. Lead with US medical learners (~1.1M physicians + ~140K residents + ~140K medical students + adjacent), then internationally (>10M global medical learners), with dermatology as the wedge. Note that visual-reasoning-intensive learning is the platform's natural extension into non-medical verticals.

02

"Why now? Why couldn't you build this 24 months ago?"

Frontier multimodal LLMs reached clinical-quality reasoning on visual tasks in 2024–2026. Image generation reached Sketchy-quality output in roughly the same window. Google's SCIN dataset for skin-of-color inclusivity dropped in 2024. None of those existed in 2023.

03

"What about UWorld or Amboss building this themselves?"

They have brand and distribution but not AI-native foundations. Bolt-on AI is structurally inferior to AI-native architecture. Migration costs are real. Neither has access to a curated medical knowledge graph or a Sketchy-equivalent generation pipeline. They will buy or partner, not build, when they recognize the threat.

04

"What if frontier models commoditize content generation?"

Generation is one component. The QA pipeline, knowledge graph grounding, clinical reviewer network, adaptive engine, and learner-experience integration are where defensibility lives. Better frontier models make dermie better, not obsolete. Treat the frontier as an exhaust pipe that powers your engine, not as your competitor.

05

"What's the unit economics?"

At scale, inference plus storage costs ~$5–15 per user per month. Subscription pricing $25–50 per month. Gross margin 70–85%. CAC dominated initially by community-driven launch, transitioning to a program-licensing motion with higher CAC but materially higher LTV. Open-weight model deployment further compresses cost at scale.

06

"How do you defend against Sketchy building their own AI mnemonic generator?"

The first-mover advantage in AI-native medical visual content is structural — by the time a competitor invests, you have eighteen months of refinement, a content library, and integrated user data. The Sketchy team is owned by a public company (Turnitin / TLM Holdings); their willingness to invest in a category-defining AI rebuild is limited by quarterly reporting incentives.

07

"Why you?"

Dual physician-builder identity is rare. Penn MD, Hopkins residency, incoming Derm-Rheumatology fellowship at BIDMC / Harvard with Adam Rodman's group. AAD DataDerm Oversight Committee and Council on Science and Research membership. Prior role as AI Clinical Fellow and Project Lead at Glass Health. You sit at the intersection of clinical credibility and AI capability in a way that few teams do.

08

"Are you a full-time founder?"

Honest answer: completing dermatology residency through June 2026, then beginning a Derm-Rheum fellowship at BIDMC / Harvard with a concurrent research appointment. Mitigate by mapping how much time you spend on the platform now and post-fellowship, acknowledging the need for a technical co-founder or first technical hire as part of use-of-funds, and framing the fellowship affiliation as a strategic asset (Rodman's group, Harvard institutional access) rather than as a distraction.

09

"What's the ask?"

$1.5–3M seed for 18–24 months of runway. Use of funds: two to three full-time technical hires, clinical reviewer compensation, initial specialty expansion (one or two specialties beyond derm to prove portability), and limited go-to-market for residency program partnerships. Be ready to articulate the milestones each tranche unlocks.

10

"What's the exit story?"

Strategic acquirers on the education side: UWorld, Amboss, Elsevier, Wolters Kluwer, McGraw-Hill. On the AI infrastructure side: Microsoft, Google (already partner), or a major EMR vendor. Comparable transactions: Osmosis acquired by Elsevier (~$200M); Amboss Series C at ~$150–200M post-money; Sketchy acquisition at hundreds of millions.

11

"What about regulatory or AI safety risk?"

Minimal direct FDA exposure: dermie is an educational tool, not patient-facing clinical decision support. Content safety is managed through the human clinical review gate before any item reaches a learner. Privacy is bounded by the educational context — no PHI flows through the platform. The platform's risk surface is closer to a CME provider than to a medical device.

06

The Performance

Three things to bring that will be remembered after you leave.

VCs see decks every day. The things that survive in their memory after the meeting are concrete, falsifiable, and demonstrate execution discipline. Bring exactly these three.

A sixty-second product demo on dermie.io

One adaptive quiz cycle. One Sketchy-style generated mnemonic. One knowledge-graph query demonstrating evidence grounding. Three demos, ninety seconds total. Working AI-native medical software is rare in their inbox. This is what gets remembered.

A one-page financial sketch — three numbers per phase

ARR, gross margin, user count. Don't pretend to forecast precisely. Say explicitly that these are scenarios, not promises, and that the deal is to validate them. Sophisticated VCs respect honesty about uncertainty more than spurious five-year DCFs.

One concrete first milestone

"In the next six months: 500 paying derm residents on the platform, three residency program LOIs, two validation studies posted as preprints, and the IM expansion shipped as proof of architecture portability." Specific, time-bound, falsifiable. This communicates execution discipline more than any slide can.

07

Errors to Avoid

Four things that will lose the room if you let them happen.

→

Don't oversell the Sketchy comparison in writing. Frame it as "comparable creative and visual quality to the leading hand-illustrated competitor in the category" rather than naming Sketchy directly in slides. Verbally fine; in writing, watch the framing for legal and reputational reasons.

→

Don't promise efficacy data you don't have yet. The published literature on adaptive learning and the Helm et al. 2026 metacognition paper support the mechanism. You do not yet have outcomes data on dermie itself. Be explicit that seed funding includes a validation study — this is exactly why we structured the AAD funding ask around psychometric validation.

→

Don't undersell the AAD Derm Tank context. If you are awarded the $10K seed at the upcoming Innovation Academy, mention it. If you submitted and are pending, mention that. Validation by AAD signals clinical-community endorsement that pure-tech competitors cannot manufacture on any timeline.

→

Don't get drawn into deep technical conversation if the VC isn't technical. Translate everything to clinical outcomes, learner experience, and business model. Save the LLM pipeline architecture and the evaluation methodology for the technical due-diligence call later — and be ready for that conversation when it comes.

The frame is dermatology as the beachhead for an AI-native medical learning operating system. The portfolio is three commercializable assets, not one. The defense is four compounding moats. The performance is three concrete artifacts. The errors to avoid are four. Walk in with that structure in hand, and you will leave the room having earned the next conversation.
