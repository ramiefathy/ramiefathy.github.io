§ I · 01

The shape of thirteen years.
Volume is a proxy for free attention.

Plot annual hours and the curve is not a taste history — it is a workload history, inverted. A few evenings of psych-rock and Coltrane in college (2013–14), then three years of silence: 1,176 days without a single logged play, the era of other apps and other priorities. The account reawakens on June 9, 2017 — two days later it logs 23.1 hours in a single day, still the all-time record — and then climbs through medical school to a staggering pandemic peak: 1,566 hours in 2020, more than four hours of music for every day of the year it played. Residency cuts it down year over year, to a 2025 floor of 238 hours — a sixth of the peak. The listening didn't change because taste changed. It changed because attention got conscripted.

The stepped line is not drawn from the biography — it is drawn from the signal. A changepoint algorithm (PELT, fitted to the monthly series with no knowledge of what the months meant) cuts these nine years into six regimes: a 43-hour-a-month baseline, a step up in February 2019 (the smart-speaker era begins), a leap to 139 hours a month in December 2019 — three months before lockdown — then two step-downs through 2020–21, and a final break in June 2022. Intern year began July 1, 2022. Asked "where did this life change?", the mathematics answered within one month of the actual start date of residency — and insisted, inconveniently, that the pandemic listening surge was already underway before the pandemic was.

§ III · 03

Three eras, three listeners.
College, medical school, residency.

Cut the archive at the biographical seams and three different people emerge. The college account is a sketch — 17 listening days of Haydn, Coltrane, and psych-rock obscurities, all on a MacBook. The medical-school account is a torrent: five years averaging 2.6 hours per listening day, music running like tap water through study sessions and a locked-down apartment. The residency account is rationed: a third of the volume per active year, squeezed into commutes and the edges of call schedules.

§ IV · 04

The pandemic year.
2020, when the apartment never went quiet.

2020 is its own geological layer. The account played music on 360 of 366 days — averaging 4.35 hours on each of them — and logged 27,193 plays across 2,623 artists, more artists than some whole eras. A streak of 339 consecutive listening days ran from late 2019 until November 1, 2020. Nearly all of it flowed through a Google Home speaker: 1,325 of 2020's hours are cast-device plays, the sound of one apartment kept permanently un-silent through lockdown.

The year also broke the discovery meter — 1,606 first-time artists, the all-time record — while simultaneously becoming more concentrated: the top-five artists' share of minutes jumped from ~15% (2018–19) to 24.8%. Both at once: a year of frantic exploration wrapped around a small comfort-core played into the ground. Khruangbin's 112.9 hours in 2020 is the largest single artist-year in the archive — more than most artists earn in a lifetime here — with Tame Impala (99.8h) and The Beatles (83.9h) right behind.

§ V · 05

Artist arcs.
Spikes, pulses, and one constant.

Stretch each top artist across the years and three distinct shapes appear. The spike-decay: Khruangbin takes 54% of its lifetime hours in a single year and never recovers; Connan Mockasin — the #5 artist of all time — collapses from 36.6 hours in 2020 to twelve minutes in 2022 and stays gone. The pulse: deadmau5 disappears and returns every two or three years like a comet, peaking once in 2018 and again in 2024. And the constant: Radiohead has logged meaningful hours in every active year since 2017 and has been the #1 artist for five consecutive years (2022–2026) — never the loudest obsession, always the floor. The all-time podium is a photo finish — Khruangbin 209.9h, Tame Impala 206.7h, Radiohead 204.7h — but only one of the three got there without a spike.

§ VI · 06

The canon.
The tracks thirteen years agreed on.

One track owns the archive. "Charlotte's Thong" — Connan Mockasin's nine-minute slow-motion guitar fever dream — has 183 finished plays and 25.5 hours of total runtime, ten hours clear of the #2. It peaked at 79 plays in 2020 alone, and it was still the top track of a month as recently as May 2026, five years after its author otherwise vanished from the rotation. The rest of the all-time list splits evenly between epics that win on duration (Stairway to Heaven, Paranoid Android) and Khruangbin grooves that win on count ("White Gloves," at 186 plays, is the most-finished track of all).

§ VII · 07

The clock that medicine reset.
Night-owl student, morning-shift resident.

Convert every timestamp to Eastern time — the clock this account actually lived on, Philadelphia then Baltimore — and the most physiological finding in the archive appears. The medical-school listener is a night owl: the curve climbs all evening, peaks at 8–9 pm, and is still doing serious volume at 1 am (143.8 hours logged in the 1 am hour alone — more than most artists' lifetime totals). The residency listener has been flipped bodily forward: the 1 am shoulder is gone, the trough lands at 4–5 am, and a new spike appears at 7 am — the commute, pre-rounding hour — followed by a midday plateau. Medical training didn't just shrink the listening; it moved it twelve hours.

§ VIII · 08

Discovery & one-era wonders.
How new things get in, and when they leave.

First-time artists per year track the breathing room in the calendar: ~900 a year through medical school, a record 1,606 in 2020, halved in the residency years — and then an odd 2025 uptick to 744, the sound of a PGY-3 with slightly more elective time. Cumulatively the account has touched 8,487 artists, but the overwhelming majority are one-night stands: of 27,266 tracks with at least one finished play, 16,095 — 59% — were finished exactly once.

§ IX · 09

The narrowing door.
Whether a discovery survives depends on when it arrived.

Group every artist by the year they first earned three real plays, then ask a colder question: what fraction of each class was still being played one, two, five years later? The answer is a retention table that any subscription business would recognize — and it is brutal about chronology. The class of 2017, the reawakening cohort that carried the pre-streaming canon back into the account, still surfaces at around 70% three years on — fading to a third only by 2025; those weren't discoveries so much as re-installations. The classes of 2018 and 2019 hold near half at one year. Then the door starts closing: the enormous 2020 class — 348 artists admitted during the pandemic flood — keeps only 24% at one year, and the residency-era classes of 2021–2023 lose roughly nine in ten by their first anniversary (2024's class is holding an only-slightly-less-bleak 20%). New music still gets in. It just doesn't get to stay.

One honest confound: later cohorts are being tested against a quieter account. An artist discovered in 2023 competes for a fraction of the listening hours an artist discovered in 2019 enjoyed, so some of the collapse is opportunity, not affection. § X · 10

The afterlife of a discovery.
Obsessions don't fade — they fall to a floor.

Take all 1,393 artists discovered between mid-2017 and mid-2024 who ever reached five plays, align them on the week of first contact, and average. The resulting curve is not a gentle decline; it is a cliff with a basement. The discovery week averages 5.1 plays; within a week the average is already down near a quarter-play — the surge is spent almost entirely inside its first seven days, too fast for weekly bins to resolve a half-life. What remains is the more interesting object: a flat residue of about 0.09 plays per week — one play every eleven weeks — that holds steady for at least two years. Tested against the data, a pure exponential loses, a power law loses; the model that wins is decay plus a permanent floor. A discovery isn't a flame that burns out. It's a flash that leaves a coal.

§ XI · 11

Repetition & binge records.
The same song, eleven times before midnight.

The single-day repeat record is a tie at eleven: Red Hot Chili Peppers' "Goodbye Angels" on April 19, 2018, and John Lennon's "Hold On" on October 17, 2020 — one a new-single fixation, the other almost too on-the-nose for week 31 of a pandemic. § XII · 12

§ XIII · 13

What plays together.
Sessions are moods, and moods have borders.

A session — every play separated by less than thirty minutes — is the archive's natural unit of mood. Take the 120 artists with the most sessions (one — a game soundtrack listened to alone — has no above-chance links and drops out), link two of the rest whenever they share a session at least twice as often as chance would predict, and let a community-detection algorithm (Louvain) carve the graph with no other information. It finds four listeners. A sprawling terracotta mainland — Tame Impala, Radiohead, Khruangbin, Gorillaz — the default rotation, where most hours actually live. A green constellation orbiting Connan Mockasin: the soft-psych, bedroom-pop register of Her's, Mild High Club, Crumb — adjacent to the mainland but reliably its own evening. A gold continent of inherited canon — Beatles, Floyd, Zeppelin, the Velvet Underground — music that arrives together because it was learned together. And, floating clean off the coast, a five-node classical island: Bach, Beethoven, Brahms, Chopin, Mozart, tightly bound to each other and to almost nothing else. The borders are the finding. Thirteen years of shuffle and autoplay never blended the island into the mainland — when the account reaches for counterpoint, it reaches with a different hand.

§ XIV · 14

The hardware of listening.
Laptop, then a speaker, then a commute.

The device column quietly explains the volume curve. College listening is all MacBook. Medical school starts split between Mac and iPhone — and then, in 2019, a Google Home arrives and everything changes: cast devices jump from 1.6 hours (2018) to 470 hours (2019) to 1,325 hours in 2020. The pandemic peak isn't headphone listening; it's a speaker filling a room for whole days, music as atmosphere rather than activity. Residency inverts it again — by 2023–24 the iPhone leads and the smart speaker fades, because the listening moved out of the home and into the drive to the hospital. All-time: cast devices 2,937h (51%), iPhone 1,955h (34%), Mac 796h (14%).

§ XV · 15

Reading the instrument panel.
What the flags say — and where they lie.

The export carries behavioral flags worth reading carefully, because some of them changed meaning over the years. The skip flag is the clearest case: it reads 0.0% for every play from 2017 through 2021, then jumps to 17–22% in 2023–24. Nobody's thumb changed that much — Spotify's logging did. Pre-2022 events simply never carried the flag. The honest statement is that modern skip behavior runs 7–9% in the most recent two years, and the historical zeroes are instrumentation, not sainthood.

The flags that are consistent tell a cleaner story. The share of plays that begin because the previous track ended — trackdone, the album-flow signature — rises from 65% (2018) to 88% (2025): less and less touching the queue, more records left to run. Shuffle collapses from 23% of plays in 2017 to 3.5% at the 2020 peak and stays low — whatever else changed, this became an albums-in-order account early and stayed one.

§ XVI · 16

The spoken word.
441 hours of talk, scheduled by training.

Podcasts (323.8h) and audiobooks (117.5h) live on their own calendar, and it is a residency calendar. Podcast hours explode exactly once: 153.4 hours in 2023 — PGY-2, the commute year — more than the four surrounding years combined. The top-shows list is a syllabus of that life: Hard Fork and The Daily for the drive, The Grenz Zone (a dermatopathology podcast, 20.6h) for board prep, Goljan lectures back in 2020 for Step study. § XVII · 17

Library vs. behavior.
The archivist and the player, still strangers.

The account-data snapshot (2026) still tells the story the previous essay found, and the thirteen-year archive sharpens it. The saved library holds 2,060 tracks across 701 artists, plus 144 playlists with 5,910 placements — and only nine followed artists. The library's top shelf is Radiohead, Pink Floyd, alt-J, Nirvana, deadmau5: the self-image. The all-time play log ranks Khruangbin first, an artist whose entire dominance fits inside three years. Pink Floyd — 54 saved tracks, #2 in the library — peaked in 2019–20 and has logged barely an hour a year since. Saving is a statement about who you think you are; playing is a record of who you were that month. Thirteen years of receipts say the archivist writes checks the player rarely cashes — and a handful of artists (Radiohead, Mac DeMarco, Tame Impala) are the only ones honored by both.

§ XVIII · 18

A diagnosis, revised.
Listening as a biomarker of attention.

One year of data diagnosed four listeners in one account. Thirteen years reveal what they have in common: every one of them is downstream of the calendar. The volume curve is medical training drawn in negative space — it explodes when a pandemic empties the schedule, drains as residency fills it, and flips its circadian phase the month the hospital starts owning the mornings. The discovery rate breathes with elective time. The devices map the floor plan of each apartment and the length of each commute. Even the obsessions are scheduled: salsa for two days of a locked-down September, dog-calming ambient the week a dog arrives, a game soundtrack in the one stretch of PGY-3 with room for a hundred-hour game.

Against all of that churn, exactly one signal refuses to move. Radiohead has scored every era of this archive — top artist of the year five years running, never the spike, never absent — the one taste that survived a college account, a three-year silence, a pandemic, and a residency intact. The previous essay called it the load-bearing favorite. The long data is blunter: it is the only thing in 5,770 hours that looks like a trait rather than a circumstance. Everything else was weather. That was climate.

Methods & honest caveats

Sources. All longitudinal figures are computed from Spotify's Extended Streaming History export — 100,098 audio events, December 2013 through June 2026, each carrying timestamp, milliseconds played, track/episode/audiobook identity, platform, shuffle/skip flags, and start/end reasons. Library, playlist, follow, and Wrapped figures in § XVII come from the separate account-data snapshot (2026) used by the previous version of this essay. Video events (a few hours total) are excluded.

Time. Spotify logs UTC. Every chart here converts to America/New_York, the clock this account's life actually ran on (Princeton, Philadelphia, Baltimore). The conversion was validated against the sleep trough, which sits at 4–5 am Eastern in every era. A previous version of § VII used America/Los_Angeles; that was wrong, and its "morning listener" reading is corrected above.

Counting rules. "Hours" sum milliseconds played, including partial plays. "Finished plays" count events of ≥30 seconds, Spotify's conventional stream threshold. Artist identity is the album-artist string as exported; track totals merge remaster/edition variants by title and artist; artist identity is otherwise as exported. Sessions split at 30 minutes of silence. The 2026 column everywhere is a partial year (through early June).

Instrumentation drift. The skip flag is absent (always false) before ~2022 and the platform string format changed several times; cross-era comparisons of those fields are avoided or explicitly flagged in § XV. The 2015–16 silence reflects platform absence, not musical silence — this archive sees only what played through Spotify.

What the export still cannot say. No genre labels, no release years, no location. The era boundaries (Penn Med, residency) are biographical knowledge applied to the data, not facts within it; the July 2022 split date is the start of intern year.

Changepoints (§ I). PELT (the ruptures implementation, least-squares cost, minimum segment four months) run on the 108-month series Jun 2017 – May 2026, penalty set by a BIC-style criterion with noise variance estimated from first differences. The six regimes shown are the algorithm's output unedited; a stiffer penalty (2× BIC) keeps the Dec 2019, Oct 2020, and Jan 2022 breaks. The algorithm received no biographical information.

Retention cohorts (§ IX). An artist joins a cohort in the calendar year of their first ≥30s play, provided they reached ≥3 such plays in that year (drive-by shuffle encounters excluded); "retained at +N years" means ≥1 finished play in that later calendar year. Cells whose target year is 2026 are blanked — the year is half-observed, and showing it would manufacture collapse. Later cohorts are also tested against fewer total listening hours; the table measures survival in a shrinking habitat, not affection alone.

Discovery decay (§ X). Artists first played Jul 2017 – Jun 2024 with ≥5 lifetime finished plays (n=1,393), aligned on first-play week and averaged over 104 weeks. Three models were fit by least squares: pure exponential, power law, and exponential plus constant floor; the floor model wins decisively (ΔAIC ≈ −188 vs the pure exponential, −187 vs the power law). The spike's fitted time constant is shorter than the one-week bin, so the essay claims only "spent within the first week," not a precise half-life. The ≥5-play filter conditions on later behavior — only about 22% of first-time artists clear it — so the floor describes discoveries that took hold at all, not the median encounter. Fitting individual artists instead of the population fails for the reasons § X illustrates.

Session graph (§ XIII). Nodes are the 119 artists appearing in the most 30-minute-gap sessions; an edge requires ≥5 shared sessions and co-occurrence ≥2× the rate expected from each artist's session frequency alone (a pointwise-mutual-information-style filter that removes "both are simply everywhere" links; the chance baseline counts all sessions including music-free ones, which slightly loosens the filter — a bias that works against, not for, the separation shown). Communities are Louvain partitions on edge weights (modularity 0.34, fixed seed); the layout runs force-direction within each community and tiles the communities apart for legibility — only the colors and links carry meaning.

Calendar (§ II). Each cell is one Eastern-time calendar day; shading thresholds are the 25th/50th/75th/90th percentiles of all 2,573 nonzero listening days, so the palette is calibrated to the archive's own distribution rather than to a fixed scale. Tooling for all of the above: Python (scipy, ruptures, networkx, python-louvain); charts are precomputed and inlined as static SVG — this page runs no script.
