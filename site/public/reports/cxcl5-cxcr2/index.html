§ 02 · Mechanism

The pathway, in pictures.

How the CXCL5/CXCR2 axis recruits neutrophils into skin, why that drives pyoderma gangrenosum and Sweet syndrome, and exactly where each drug class interrupts it. Step through the four states.

Disease state — PG & Sweet syndrome

A trigger makes keratinocytes and interferon-activated dermal fibroblasts pour out ELR⁺ CXC chemokines (CXCL5 and its relatives). These form a chemical trail that pulls CXCR2-bearing neutrophils out of the blood and into the skin, where they swarm and destroy tissue — the ulcer of PG, the plaques of Sweet. The neutrophils then release more IL-1β/TNF, feeding the loop.

CXCL5 & ELR⁺ CXC chemokines (the "come here" signal) Neutrophils (the cells that cause damage) Receptor / pathway intact Tissue damage / disease lesion
In plain words

Think of CXCL5 as a scent trail and neutrophils (a type of white blood cell) as bloodhounds. In PG and Sweet syndrome, the skin lays down a massively strong scent trail, so far too many neutrophils rush in and tear up healthy tissue. CXCR2 is the dog's nose — the receptor that smells the trail.

There are two ways to break this up. Eltrekibart is an antibody that mops up the scent itself (the chemokines), so there's nothing to follow. The CXCR2 antagonists (danirixin, reparixin, AZD5069, navarixin, ladarixin, SX-682) plug the dog's nose, so the trail is still there but the neutrophils can't smell it. Either way, fewer neutrophils flood the skin.

§ 03 · Result

The headline molecular result.

In GSE298908 — the only dataset with a directly usable count matrix containing PG, Sweet, and control samples — the CXCL5/CXCR2 axis and neutrophil modules are strongly upregulated in both diseases.

Axis & module activity vs control

PG vs control (n=58 vs 10) Sweet vs control (n=45 vs 10)
~180×≈ 2^7.53 CXCL5 in PG lesions

A log2 fold-change of 7.53 for CXCL5 in PG (and 5.33 in Sweet) is a very large effect. Interpret cautiously — this is an initial log2-CPM analysis, not the final DESeq2/limma model. Both diseases also light up the broader CXCR2-ligand module and the neutrophil module well above controls.

In plain words

We compared diseased skin to healthy skin and measured how much more of each "signal molecule" the diseased skin makes. "log2 fold-change" is just a zoom dial: every +1 means double. So +7.53 means CXCL5 is roughly 2 × 2 × 2 … (7.5 times) ≈ 180× higher in PG lesions. +5.33 in Sweet is about 40× higher. These are huge jumps.

A "module" is a group of related genes scored together so one odd gene can't fool us. Both the chemokine module and the neutrophil module are far above normal in both diseases — which is exactly what we'd expect if the pathway in the diagram above is switched on.

§ 04 · Genes

Gene-level differential expression.

The curated 36-gene panel, ranked by effect. Of the full 28,287 tested genes, 8,891 pass FDR<0.05 in PG and 7,561 in Sweet. Toggle the contrast; hover any bar for the exact effect.

CXC chemokine ligands (CXCL1/2/3/5/6/8), S100A8/9/12 alarmins, and Fc-receptor / neutrophil-granule genes dominate the upregulated end. CXCL12 and ELANE move down — consistent with an acute neutrophil-recruitment program rather than resident homeostasis.

In plain words

Each bar is one gene; the further right, the more over-active it is in disease (bars to the left are switched down). The genes pushed up the most are the whole "recruit-the-neutrophils" toolkit — the scent molecules (CXCL family), the alarm proteins (S100A8/9/12), and neutrophil machinery. That's not random noise: it's the same story as the diagram, told gene by gene.

"FDR < 0.05" is a strictness filter that controls false alarms — it means we'd expect fewer than 5% of the flagged genes to be flukes. About 8,900 genes in PG and 7,600 in Sweet clear that bar, so the signal is broad, not a one-gene coincidence.

§ 05 · Candidates

Candidate scorecard.

Drugs ranked by a prioritization heuristic — not a clinical-efficacy estimate. Eltrekibart is the cleanest mechanistic benchmark (directly blocks the ELR+ CXC family incl. CXCL5); the CXCR2/CXCR1-2 antagonists are repurposing candidates pending signature-reversal analysis.

Drug ▲▼ Tier ▲▼ Mechanistic ▲▼ Human exp. ▲▼ Safety ▲▼ Total ▲▼ Interpretation

Disease-signature-reversal score is fixed at 1 across all candidates — it is the explicit gap this run does not yet fill (requires LINCS/CMap reversal). That is why ranking leans on mechanism + human exposure today.

In plain words

This table is a shortlist, not a verdict. We scored each drug on four simple questions: Does it hit the right target? Has it already been given to humans (so safety is partly known)? Can we show it reverses the disease's gene pattern? Is it reasonably safe? Higher total = more worth a closer look.

One column — "does it reverse the disease signature" — is set to 1 for everyone on purpose, because we haven't done that experiment yet. So today's ranking mostly rewards drugs that hit the target cleanly and already have human data. Eltrekibart tops the list as the benchmark; the CXCR2 blockers are credible repurposing candidates waiting on the reversal test.

In plain words

This section asks "where have these drugs actually touched humans, and what do people already say about them?" ClinicalTrials.gov is the public registry of human trials, PubMed is the medical-paper database, and FAERS/openFDA is the FDA's side-effect reporting system.

The takeaway: these drugs have been tested in people (mostly for lung disease, cancer, and a hidradenitis trial for eltrekibart), but almost nobody has studied them in PG or Sweet syndrome yet. The empty FDA side-effect search doesn't mean "safe" or "doesn't work" — it just means these drug-disease pairs are nearly uncharted, which is the whole point of proposing the study.

§ 07 · Data

Dataset registry & omics readiness.

15 public GEO datasets were frozen — a snapshot of availability at run time. Only GSE298908 had a directly usable count matrix and was analyzed; the rest are sourced but await dataset-specific curation.

AccessionAssayRoleSamplesStatusTitle
In plain words

A "dataset" (GSE…) is one research group's freely shared molecular measurements from real patient skin, stored in a public library called GEO. We catalogued 15 relevant ones and "froze" a snapshot so the study is reproducible later.

The assay column is the measuring technique: bulk RNA-seq = average gene activity across a whole tissue chunk; single-cell / snRNA-seq = activity cell-by-cell; spatial = activity mapped to its exact location in the tissue; array = an older chip-based readout. Only one dataset (GSE298908) came ready-to-use, so it's the only one analyzed so far. The other 14 are downloaded and waiting — each needs hand-matching of which sample is which before it can be modeled.

§ 11 · Caveat

The important caveat & what's next.

This is a first-pass public-data analysis — not the final statistical model

The DE layer uses log2-CPM + a Welch-style normal approximation — fast, transparent, and adequate for a first signal check given how large the effect is. It is not the method a manuscript would use. Frame the result as:

Say"This supports prioritizing CXCL5/CXCR2-axis blockade for further study."
Don't say"These drugs work for neutrophilic dermatoses."
In plain words — what we can and can't say yet

We looked at which molecules are over-active in the skin and found the neutrophil-recruitment pathway is strongly switched on in PG and Sweet. That is real and encouraging — it's a good reason to study drugs that shut this pathway down.

But "the pathway is active" is not the same as "blocking it cures the disease." Proving that needs the next steps: a more careful re-analysis with the gold-standard tools, the drug-fingerprint reversal test, and finally checking real patient outcomes. Until then the honest claim is "worth studying," not "it works."

§ 12 · Reference

Plain-language glossary.

Every technical term in this report, translated.

The pathway CXCL5 / CXCR2 axis

CXCL5 and its chemical relatives are "come-here" signals (chemokines). CXCR2 is the matching receptor — the antenna — on neutrophils. Signal + antenna = neutrophils get pulled into the skin.

Neutrophil

The most common white blood cell and the body's first responder to infection. Useful in moderation; in PG and Sweet syndrome they flood in by mistake and damage healthy skin.

ELR⁺ CXC chemokines

The specific family of "come-here" signals (CXCL1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8) that all act through CXCR2. "ELR⁺" is a small chemical tag they share. Eltrekibart neutralizes this whole family.

Pyoderma gangrenosum PG

A rare skin disease causing rapidly enlarging, deep, painful ulcers — driven by neutrophils swarming the skin.

Sweet syndrome

Also called acute febrile neutrophilic dermatosis: sudden tender red plaques with fever, again driven by neutrophil flooding.

log2 fold-change log2FC

A zoom dial for "how much more." Each +1 = doubled. +7.5 ≈ 180× more; −1 = halved. Lets us compare disease skin to healthy skin on one scale.

Effect size

How big a difference is, accounting for noise — not just "is it different" but "by how much, reliably." Bigger = more striking.

p-value & FDR

The p-value is the chance a result is a fluke. FDR (false-discovery rate) is the same idea adjusted for testing thousands of genes at once. "FDR < 0.05" = fewer than 5% expected to be flukes.

Gene module

A pre-chosen group of related genes scored as one unit, so a single weird gene can't drive the conclusion. We tracked a chemokine module and a neutrophil module.

Meta-analysis

Combining results across samples (and eventually datasets) into one pooled estimate, so the answer rests on more than a single measurement.

Differential expression DE

The core comparison: which genes are turned up or down in diseased skin versus healthy skin.

log2-CPM + Welch approximation

The quick, transparent method used for this first pass. CPM ("counts per million") puts samples on a fair footing; the Welch step is a simple, robust way to test differences. Good for a fast signal check — not the final method.

DESeq2 / limma / edgeR

The gold-standard statistical tools for this kind of data, used in the manuscript-grade follow-up. More careful about noise and study design than the quick first-pass method.

scanpy

The standard toolkit for analyzing single-cell data — needed for the cell-by-cell datasets that aren't yet processed.

Bulk vs single-cell vs spatial

Bulk = average over a whole tissue piece. Single-cell / snRNA-seq = read each cell separately. Spatial = keep each reading's exact location in the tissue map.

GEO & GSE accession

GEO is a public library of shared molecular datasets. A "GSE…" number is one dataset's catalog ID (like an ISBN for a study's data).

Count matrix

The raw spreadsheet of how many times each gene was detected in each sample — the input to the analysis.

Signature reversal LINCS / CMap

A database trick: find drugs whose gene-activity "fingerprint" is the mirror image of the disease's. If a drug undoes the disease pattern, it's a strong repurposing lead. This is the test we haven't run yet.

ChEMBL

A public database of drugs and exactly which biological targets they hit — used to confirm each candidate's mechanism.

FAERS / openFDA

The FDA's public side-effect reporting system. A signal source only — it can hint at safety patterns but cannot prove a drug works.

Target-trial emulation

A rigorous way to mimic a randomized trial using existing health records — comparing similar real patients who did vs didn't get a drug. The planned validation step.

All of Us / TriNetX / claims

Large real-world health databases (research cohorts, hospital networks, insurance records) where the future validation study could look for whether these drugs affect PG/Sweet risk.

Repurposing

Using an existing, already-tested drug for a new disease — faster and cheaper than inventing one from scratch.

mAb vs small-molecule antagonist

Eltrekibart is an antibody — a designed protein that grabs and neutralizes the chemokine signals. The CXCR2 antagonists are small molecules that plug the receptor. Two routes to the same goal: fewer neutrophils recruited.
